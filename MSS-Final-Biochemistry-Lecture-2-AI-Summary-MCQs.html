Structure and cells

Basics

Functions of bone

Spot it: Bone is a connective tissue and the body’s calcium bank.

  • Mechanical: levers and attachment for muscle; load bearing. Needs tensile and compressive strength.
  • Protective: skull, ribs and vertebrae shield brain, thoracic organs and spinal cord. Marrow houses haematopoiesis.
  • Metabolic: reservoir of 99% of Ca, 85% of phosphate, 55% of Mg. Buffers acid. Secretes osteocalcin.

Matrix

Bone matrix: organic and inorganic

Spot it: Collagen without mineral bends (rickets); mineral with poor collagen is brittle (OI).

  • Organic (osteoid), ~40%: flexibility and tensile strength.
    • Type I collagen (~90% of organic): triple helix, Gly-X-Y repeats.
    • Proteoglycans: resist compression.
    • Non-collagen proteins: osteocalcin, osteonectin, osteopontin.
    • Growth factors and cytokines (TGF-β, IGF, BMPs) stored in the matrix.
  • Inorganic, ~60%: rigidity and hardness. Calcium hydroxyapatite Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂, deposited in gaps between collagen fibrils. Crystal growth is regulated by osteocalcin, osteonectin and alkaline phosphatase.
Matrix

Type I collagen: from gene to mineral

Spot it: Scurvy = weak collagen (vitamin C); lysyl oxidase needs copper; ALP supplies phosphate for mineral.

  1. Pro-α chains made in the osteoblast ER; glycine at every third residue.
  2. Hydroxylation: prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases need vitamin C and Fe²⁺.
  3. Triple helix: procollagen is secreted; N- and C-propeptides are cleaved.
  4. Cross-links: lysyl oxidase (copper) forms pyridinoline cross-links.
  5. Mineralisation: ALP cleaves pyrophosphate (PPi) to Pi; Ca and Pi nucleate as hydroxyapatite.
Matrix

Non-collagenous proteins

Spot it: Gla protein needing vitamin K = osteocalcin; “bridge” between matrix and osteoclast = osteopontin.

  • Osteocalcin (BGLAP): 49-amino-acid protein hormone in bone and dentin. Secreted by osteoblasts. Has Gla domains, so synthesis is vitamin K dependent. Binds hydroxyapatite and regulates mineralisation.
  • Osteonectin: calcium-binding glycoprotein from osteoblasts. Binds collagen and bone mineral; initiates mineralisation and crystal formation.
  • Osteopontin: phosphoprotein and glycoprotein. A regulatory and signalling molecule, not structural. “Bridge” between collagen matrix and osteoclasts. Made by osteoblasts, osteocytes, osteoclasts, macrophages and other immune cells.

Osteocytes are osteoblasts trapped in bone; they sense mechanical load and regulate remodelling. Progenitors differentiate into the osteoblast lineage.

Bone remodelling

Process

Bone turnover cycle

Spot it: Activation → Resorption → Reversal → Formation → Quiescence (“ARF”).

  1. Activation: remodelling starts at a site (basic multicellular unit).
  2. Resorption: osteoclasts attach and dissolve mineral and matrix.
  3. Reversal: mononuclear cells clean the surface and prepare it for new bone.
  4. Formation: osteoblasts lay down osteoid; calcium phosphate then mineralises it.
  5. Quiescence: the site rests until the next cycle.

In health, formation equals resorption (“zero balance”). Osteoblasts have receptors for PTH, calcitonin, vitamin D, cytokines and growth factors.

Regulators

  • Stimulate both cell types: PTH and 1,25-(OH)₂ vitamin D.
  • Inhibit osteoblasts: corticosteroids.
  • Inhibit osteoclasts: calcitonin and oestrogen.

Calcium

Calcium metabolism

Spot it: Only ionised (free) calcium is active.

  • Skeleton holds 99%, as hydroxyapatite. Plasma 2.2–2.6 mmol/L. Urinary excretion 2.5–10 mmol/24 h.
  • Plasma Ca: 50% ionised (active), 40% protein-bound (mainly albumin), 10% complexed with anions (HCO₃⁻, phosphate, citrate, lactate).
  • Organs: skeleton, gut, kidney. Calcitropic hormones: PTH, calcitonin, vitamin D, PTHrP.
  • Inside cells: muscle contraction, hormone secretion, glycogen metabolism, cell division.
  • Outside cells: bone mineralisation, blood clotting, nerve signalling and other functions.
Disorder

Hypocalcaemia

Spot it: Check albumin first: low total Ca may be low protein-bound Ca only.

Low total Ca from reduced albumin-bound Ca, reduced free Ca, or both.

  • Hypoalbuminaemia
  • Chronic renal failure
  • Magnesium deficiency
  • Hypoparathyroidism and pseudohypoparathyroidism
  • Rickets and osteomalacia (vitamin D deficiency or resistance)
  • Acute haemorrhagic pancreatitis
  • Hungry bone syndrome: healing phase after treatment of hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism or haematological malignancy bone disease
Disorder

Hypercalcaemia

Spot it: Most often overactive parathyroid glands or malignancy.

Flux of Ca into extracellular fluid (from skeleton, gut or kidney) exceeds efflux.

  • Mechanisms: increased gut absorption, renal retention, skeletal resorption, or a combination.
  • Causes: overactive parathyroids, cancer, other medical disorders, some drugs, excess Ca or vitamin D supplements.
  • Effects: weak bones, kidney stones, heart and brain dysfunction.
Phosphate

Phosphate metabolism

Spot it: PTH is the main regulator; phosphate is less tightly controlled than calcium.

  • Plasma Pi as H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻; normal 0.9–1.3 mmol/L.
  • Inorganic Pi is a main component of hydroxyapatite. Inside cells it is mostly organic: nucleic acids, phospholipids, phosphoproteins, ATP, cyclic nucleotides.
  • Absorbed in the gut and excreted by the kidney.
Disorder

Hypo- and hyperphosphataemia

Spot it: Insulin shifts Pi into cells; CKD is the commonest cause of high Pi.

Hypophosphataemia

  • Low Pi impairs ATP formation, glycolysis and cell function.
  • Causes: shift into cells (high insulin), renal wasting, reduced absorption (antacids, malabsorption), loss from cells (acidosis, catabolism).
  • Also: excess PTH, Fanconi syndrome, X-linked hypophosphataemic rickets, tumour-induced osteomalacia.

Hyperphosphataemia

  • Usually from inability of the kidney to excrete Pi: chronic kidney disease (commonest).
  • Higher renal threshold in hypoparathyroidism, pseudohypoparathyroidism, acromegaly.
  • Also: high dietary Pi, excess vitamin D, infection, cell destruction (chemotherapy).
  • A rapid rise may cause hypocalcaemia: tetany, seizures, hypotension.
Magnesium

Magnesium metabolism and disorders

Spot it: Low Mg = more neuromuscular excitability, because less Mg blocks calcium entry.

  • Fourth most abundant cation. By compartment: total body = Ca; intracellular = K; extracellular = Na. Mg is the most abundant intracellular divalent cation. 55% in skeleton, 45% intracellular.
  • Cofactor for >300 enzymes; forms Mg-ATP; allosteric activator.
  • Hypomagnesaemia (<1.46 mg/dL): GI or renal loss. Low Mg lets more Ca enter nerve endings, so more neurotransmitter release and increased excitability.
  • Hypermagnesaemia (>2.6 mg/dL): excess intake (antacids, enemas, Mg-containing IV fluids).

Calcitropic hormones

Hormone

Parathyroid hormone (PTH)

Spot it: Low Ca → high PTH → Ca up, phosphate down.

  • Synthesis: pre-pro-PTH (115 aa) → ER removes signal peptide → pro-PTH (90 aa) → Golgi removes 6 aa → PTH (84 aa) stored in chief-cell granules.
  • Bone: increases resorption and mobilises Ca (continuous PTH via RANKL). Over time both osteoclasts and osteoblasts are stimulated, with osteoclasts predominating. Intermittent PTH is anabolic.
  • Kidney: ↑ Ca reabsorption (distal tubule); ↓ phosphate reabsorption (proximal tubule), so phosphaturia; ↑ 1α-hydroxylase, so more calcitriol.
  • Gut: indirect, through calcitriol.
  • Net: plasma Ca ↑, Pi ↓.

Control of secretion

  • Ionised Ca by negative feedback (calcium-sensing receptor): low Ca raises PTH, high Ca lowers it.
  • High 1,25-(OH)₂D inhibits PTH transcription.
  • High phosphate stimulates PTH by lowering Ca and inhibiting calcitriol formation.
Hormone

Vitamin D (calcitriol)

Spot it: Skin → liver (25-OH) → kidney (1α-hydroxylase) = active 1,25-(OH)₂D.

  • Skin: sunlight (UV) converts 7-dehydrocholesterol to cholecalciferol (D₃). Diet also supplies vitamin D.
  • Liver: 25-hydroxylase makes 25-OH-D₃.
  • Kidney (proximal tubule): 1α-hydroxylase makes 1,25-(OH)₂D₃ (calcitriol).
  • Actions: ↑ Ca and Pi absorption from gut; ↑ Ca reabsorption in kidney; in bone it mobilises Ca and Pi by increasing mature osteoclasts but also stimulates osteoblasts. Net effect: Ca mobilisation.

Hormone

Calcitonin

Spot it: Secreted when Ca is high; opposes PTH.

  • 32-amino-acid peptide from thyroid parafollicular C cells.
  • Secreted when plasma Ca is high, so plasma levels track plasma Ca.
  • Directly inhibits osteoclasts (less resorption) and increases renal Ca excretion. Lowers plasma Ca and Pi.
  • Used as a treatment for hypercalcaemia (and Paget disease).
  • Stimulants: β-adrenergic agonists, dopamine, oestrogen, gastrin, CCK, glucagon, secretin.

Bone disorders

Metabolic

Rickets and osteomalacia

Spot it: Soft, bowed bones with low or normal Ca, low Pi, high ALP and high PTH = vitamin D lack.

  • Rickets: vitamin D deficiency in childhood. Weak, bowed weight-bearing bones, dental defects, hypocalcaemia.
  • Osteomalacia: vitamin D deficiency in adults; demineralised bone, especially in women with little sun exposure.
  • Causes of deficiency: reduced intake or production (sun, diet, malabsorption); reduced processing (liver, kidney); increased renal loss.
  • Types: vitamin D deficient; hypophosphataemic.
  • Treatment: vitamin D plus calcium.
Metabolic

Osteoporosis

Spot it: Less bone, but the mineral-to-matrix ratio is normal; labs are normal.

  • Progressive loss of bone mass per unit volume, causing skeletal weakness. Remaining bone has a normal mineral:organic ratio.
  • The most common metabolic bone disease. Linked to ageing and menopause (oestrogen loss increases RANKL and osteoclast lifespan).
  • Low Ca intake adds to low bone density, early loss and fractures.
  • Primary (postmenopausal) or secondary (nutrition, endocrine causes).
  • Treatment: bisphosphonates or denosumab.
Endocrine

Hyperparathyroidism

Spot it: High Ca, low Pi, high ALP and bone resorption (osteitis fibrosa cystica).

  • One or more parathyroid glands overactive; excess PTH raises Ca (hypercalcaemia) and causes bone resorption.
  • Primary: autonomous PTH. Treat by parathyroidectomy.
  • Secondary (e.g. CKD-MBD): low calcitriol, high Pi, low Ca, high PTH.
Genetic

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI)

Spot it: Brittle bones from a Type I collagen defect; autosomal dominant.

  • Brittle bone disease: abnormal fragility of bones.
  • >90% have mutations in the genes for Type I collagen (COL1A1/COL1A2). Most common pattern is autosomal dominant.
  • Deficient or abnormal Type I collagen, with loss of the triple helix.
Genetic

Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (EDS)

Spot it: Stretchy skin and loose joints from faulty fibrillar collagen.

  • Heterogeneous group of connective tissue disorders: heritable defects in fibrillar collagen metabolism.
  • Deficient collagen-processing enzymes (lysyl hydroxylase, N-procollagen peptidase) or mutations in collagen types I, III and V.
  • Usually autosomal dominant.
Metabolic

Paget disease of bone

Spot it: Age over 50, bone pain or enlargement, very high ALP, normal Ca and Pi.

  • Bone enlargement and thickening from increased osteoclast and osteoblast activity (high turnover). Cause unknown (racial and possibly viral factors).
  • Clinical: male = female, age >50; ache, not severe unless fracture or tumour.
  • Investigations: characteristic X-ray; ALP markedly raised; urinary hydroxyproline raised.
  • Treatment: bisphosphonates, calcitonin.

High-yield summary

  • Bone holds 99% Ca, 85% Pi, 55% Mg.
  • Matrix: 40% organic (Type I collagen ~90%) and 60% hydroxyapatite.
  • Osteocalcin is vitamin K dependent (Gla). Osteopontin bridges matrix and osteoclasts.
  • Collagen needs vitamin C (hydroxylation) and copper (lysyl oxidase).
  • Osteoblast: ALP, osteocalcin. Osteoclast: TRAP, cathepsin K, RANKL-driven.
  • Remodelling: activation, resorption, reversal, formation, quiescence.
  • PTH: ↑ Ca, ↓ Pi, ↑ calcitriol. Calcitonin: ↓ Ca (inhibits osteoclasts).
  • Calcitriol: skin → liver 25-OH → kidney 1α-hydroxylase; ↑ gut Ca and Pi.
  • FGF23 (osteocytes): ↓ Pi reabsorption and ↓ 1α-hydroxylase.
  • Low Mg → more excitability. High Pi → low Ca → more PTH.
  • Rickets: ↓ Ca, ↓ Pi, ↑ ALP, ↑ PTH. Osteoporosis: normal labs. Paget: ↑↑ ALP.
  • OI: Type I collagen, AD. EDS: collagen processing, mostly AD.
