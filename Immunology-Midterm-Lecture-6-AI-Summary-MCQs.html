1. What T and B cells recognize

A B-cell receptor (BCR) binds an intact antigen directly: it might be a protein surface, a polysaccharide, or another accessible structure. The BCR is membrane immunoglobulin (Ig) with signalling partners Igα and Igβ (cluster of differentiation [CD] 79a and 79b). Plasma cells secrete antibodies of the same basic specificity. Conventional alpha–beta T-cell receptors (TCRs) instead recognize the combined surface of a peptide and a major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecule. TCRs remain membrane-bound. The CD3 signalling complex and associated zeta chains carry immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) that transmit the signal.

Most T cells have alpha–beta receptors; gamma–delta T cells are a minority enriched at some epithelial barriers. Other exceptions to the conventional peptide rule include invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells, which see lipids on CD1d, and mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells, which see microbial metabolites on MHC class I-related protein 1 (MR1).

Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) is the human MHC system: HLA-A, -B and -C are class I; HLA-DP, -DQ and -DR are class II. HLA typing helps assess donor–recipient compatibility; antibody screening and crossmatching answer related but distinct questions. Disease associations are risk markers, not diagnoses or proof of causation. Class I can also be found on platelets; mature red cells lack conventional class I. Class II has membrane-spanning alpha and beta chains, while class I has a membrane-spanning heavy chain and noncovalently associated beta-2 microglobulin. The lecture also illustrates that some engineered TCR therapies, such as afamitresgene autoleucel (afami-cel), recognize targets only with specified HLA alleles.

2. How a naïve T cell becomes activated

  1. Recognition: TCR binds the correct peptide–MHC. CD4 or CD8 binds the corresponding MHC class and supports signalling through CD3. Dendritic cells are the usual primers of naïve T cells; macrophages and B cells can present to appropriate T cells in other contexts.
  2. Costimulation: CD28 on the T cell binds B7 (CD80/CD86) on the APC. Infection increases B7. Recognition without adequate costimulation can leave a naïve T cell unresponsive (anergy). Adhesion, such as lymphocyte function-associated antigen 1 (LFA-1) binding intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), stabilizes the immune synapse.
  3. Instruction and expansion: Local cytokines steer differentiation. Activated T cells produce interleukin-2 (IL-2) and upregulate its receptor, including CD25, to drive clonal expansion. Autocrine means the secreting cell responds to its own signal; nearby cells may also respond.

Feedback: Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) restrains costimulation by binding B7. CD40 ligand (CD40L, also CD154) on helper T cells binds CD40 to activate macrophages, license dendritic cells, or help B cells, depending on the partner. Macrophages can release IL-1, IL-6, IL-12 and tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α); activated type 1 helper T cells release interferon gamma (IFN-γ).

Superantigens bypass ordinary peptide specificity

The tuberculin skin-test vignette illustrates a delayed T-cell response. CD28–B7 supports naïve T-cell priming; the swelling on re-exposure reflects a memory response and local recruitment, rather than an immediate antibody reaction.

A superantigen bridges MHC class II outside the peptide groove to certain variable beta (Vβ) regions of TCRs. Many unrelated clones carrying the relevant Vβ region activate together. T-cell IFN-γ and other mediators stimulate myeloid cells, which release IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-α; excessive systemic inflammation can cause fever, rash and hypotension. Examples: staphylococcal enterotoxins, toxic shock syndrome toxin 1 (TSST-1), and certain streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins.

3. Helper T cells choose an effector programme

A mature naïve CD4 T cell (sometimes called Th0 in the notes) expands after priming in lymph nodes or spleen. The local cytokine environment and antigen context shape its fate. These are useful teaching patterns; individual cells and tissues can vary.

4. How B cells make better antibodies

B cells mature in bone marrow and naïve cells circulate through lymphoid follicles. The BCR binds antigen through its variable region. After activation, an early extrafollicular response can rapidly supply antibody; a germinal-centre response develops more slowly and improves antibody quality.

FeatureT-dependentT-independent
Typical triggerProtein: the B cell binds and internalizes it, then presents its peptide on class II to a cognate Tfh cell.Repetitive polysaccharides cross-link many BCRs; microbial molecules such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) can also supply innate signals.
Second signalCD40L on Tfh binds CD40 on B cell, with cytokines such as IL-21.Receptor clustering and/or innate signals, without cognate T-cell help.
Typical resultClass switching, affinity maturation, memory B cells and long-lived plasma cells.Rapid, mainly immunoglobulin M (IgM); generally limited switching, affinity maturation and durable memory. Weak in young infants; splenic marginal-zone cells matter.

Linked recognition: In a conjugate vaccine, the BCR binds the bacterial sugar, takes up the attached carrier protein, and presents carrier peptide to the Tfh cell. The T cell supplies help while the B cell makes antibody against the sugar. This is why conjugates can work well in infants. Examples: Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), pneumococcal conjugates and quadrivalent meningococcal conjugates against groups A, C, W and Y (MenACWY).

Germinal centre: mutate, test, repeat

  1. Dark zone: B cells proliferate and activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID) promotes somatic hypermutation in immunoglobulin variable genes.
  2. Light zone: Follicular dendritic cells hold intact antigen for B cells to capture. B cells that capture it well can present more peptide to Tfh cells and receive survival signals. Poor competitors often die by apoptosis.
  3. Outcome: Repeated selection raises average binding affinity. AID also enables class-switch recombination: change the heavy-chain constant region and effector function while retaining the rearranged antigen-binding region. Cytokines influence the class (for example IL-4 favours IgE and TGF-β can favour immunoglobulin A [IgA]). Class switching can also occur outside germinal centres.

Outputs include memory B cells and long-lived bone marrow plasma cells. CD40L deficiency can cause X-linked hyper-IgM syndrome: poor class switching and impaired CD40-dependent immune functions. The slide’s infant with encapsulated bacterial infections plus Pneumocystis pneumonia illustrates why this defect goes beyond antibodies: helper T-cell communication with APCs, including macrophages, is also impaired. Follicular lymphoma and Burkitt lymphoma can arise from germinal-centre lineage cells.

Four useful B-cell populations

Follicular: recirculate and drive most T-dependent responses. Marginal zone: splenic cells that rapidly respond to blood-borne polysaccharides. B-1: classically described in body cavities, especially in mice; provide broadly reactive natural IgM before deliberate immunization. Natural antibodies contribute to early defence and removal of dying cells. Regulatory B cells: can release IL-10 and dampen inflammation.

5. Killing infected cells and matching the defence

Naïve CD8 cells are primed by peptide–class I, costimulation and cytokines, often on dendritic cells. A CD4 cell can license the dendritic cell through CD40L–CD40, improving CD8 priming. A differentiated cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) recognizes the matching peptide–class I on a target and can kill by two routes:

  • Perforin and granzymes: focused granule release lets granzymes access the target cytosol and trigger programmed cell death, including through caspases.
  • Fas ligand (FasL) and Fas: binding activates a target death-receptor pathway.

A CTL can detach and kill another target, and can secrete IFN-γ and TNF. Perforin defects can cause familial haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) because defective killing permits prolonged immune activation.

Exhaustion: Persistent antigen in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B virus (HBV) or tumours can reduce proliferation, cytokine release and killing. Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) binding its ligands PD-L1 or PD-L2 provides inhibitory signalling. In some cancers, antibodies such as pembrolizumab and nivolumab block PD-1 and can restore some activity. PD-1 expression alone does not prove exhaustion. Tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy expands tumour-derived cells before reinfusion (example: lifileucel in melanoma). Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-specific T cells can target infected cells in selected cases of post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD).

6. Contraction, memory and changing antigens

Naïve cells have matured without meeting their cognate antigen. Effector clones expand during infection, then most die during contraction (the deck illustrates roughly 90%, not a fixed fraction). Surviving memory cells respond faster on re-exposure. IL-7 and IL-15 help maintain many memory T-cell populations without ongoing antigen; long-lived plasma cells use specialized survival niches.

Tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM)Remain in tissues such as skin, lung and gut. Often express CD69; some express CD103. Positioned for local defence.
Memory B cells and plasma cellsMemory B cells respond on re-exposure; long-lived plasma cells continually secrete antibody without requiring a new exposure.

Durability: The slide’s estimated antibody half-lives (years) are tetanus 11, diphtheria 19, varicella-zoster 50, vaccinia 92, rubella 114, mumps 542 and measles 3,014. These are model estimates from antibody measurements, not individual guarantees or literal observation over millennia. Neutralization may decline enough to permit infection while memory still limits severe disease. A correlate of protection is a measurable marker associated with protection; slide examples include antibody to hepatitis B surface antigen (anti-HBs) at 10 milli-international units per millilitre and tetanus antitoxin at 0.1 international units per millilitre in the relevant testing contexts.

Imprinting and drift: The first strain encountered can bias recall toward shared epitopes. Mutations in variable epitopes can reduce recognition; conserved sites, such as the influenza haemagglutinin stem, are candidates for broader vaccines. Infection plus vaccination is called hybrid immunity and can broaden responses; it is not a reason to seek infection. The World Health Organization (WHO) makes seasonal influenza composition recommendations twice yearly. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) strain example in the deck is season-specific, not a permanent formulation.

Live attenuatedLimited replication mimics infection and can induce antibody and cellular immunity. Some live vaccines are contraindicated in pregnancy or severe immunodeficiency.
Inactivated, subunit or toxoidDeliver killed organism, selected antigens or inactivated toxin. Generally usable in immunocompromised patients, though responses may be weaker. Often rely on adjuvants or boosters.
ConjugateLinks polysaccharide to protein to recruit T-cell help and improve infant responses and memory.
Messenger ribonucleic acid in lipid nanoparticles (mRNA–LNP) or viral vectorHost cells make antigen, permitting class I presentation as well as antibody responses.

Passive antibodiesNirsevimab and clesrovimab illustrate passive protection against respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). Supplied antibody can protect immediately but does not generate the recipient’s own antigen-specific memory.

Newborns: Maternal IgG crosses the placenta, especially in the third trimester. It wanes over the first months as infant antibody production increases. Maternal vaccination can protect the newborn through transferred IgG. Infant vaccine timing and boosters vary by jurisdiction and product; the lecture’s examples include early primary series, influenza vaccination, pertussis vaccination in pregnancy and tetanus–diphtheria boosters.

Young children: Weak responses to plain polysaccharides before roughly age two explain the use of conjugates. Older adults: Thymic output declines rather than stopping abruptly, naïve pools shrink, and responses can weaken (immunosenescence). Chronic low-grade inflammation is sometimes called inflammaging. Higher-dose or adjuvanted vaccines are examples of strategies to improve protection in older adults.

8. Localize the immune defect from the case

Case A: recurrent otitis and pneumonia after 6–8 months, absent tonsils, very low B cells

X-linked agammaglobulinaemia (XLA) due to loss of Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK). B-cell maturation stalls, so CD19-positive B cells and multiple immunoglobulin classes are very low while T cells are generally preserved. Encapsulated bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae fit. Maternal IgG initially masks the defect. Immunoglobulin replacement is central; live vaccine decisions require specialist guidance.

Case B: cardiac outflow defect, hypocalcaemic seizures, candidiasis and low T cells

22q11.2 deletion syndrome (DiGeorge phenotype): thymic hypoplasia explains low CD3-positive T cells and viral/fungal susceptibility; hypoparathyroidism explains low calcium; conotruncal defects complete the pattern. Severity varies. Complete congenital athymia may be treated with thymus tissue implantation. Irradiated cellular blood products may be indicated in suspected severe T-cell deficiency to prevent transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease.

Compare: B cells mature in bone marrow and bind intact antigen; their effector output is antibody. T cells mature in the thymus; conventional T cells see peptide–MHC and act through cytokines, help or killing. Memory follows both arms.
