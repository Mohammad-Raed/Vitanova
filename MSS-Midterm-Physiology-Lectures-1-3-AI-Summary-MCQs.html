Lecture 1

Motor Units, Force & the Neuromuscular Junction

Motor unit

  • One alpha motor neuron and all the extrafusal skeletal-muscle fibres it innervates.
  • A typical adult extrafusal fibre has one neuromuscular junction (NMJ); this is the usual arrangement, not a rule for every specialized muscle fibre.
  • A motor unit is the smallest unit ordinarily recruited by the nervous system. Its force still depends on firing rate and muscle length.

Recruitment

  • Henneman size principle: with increasing common synaptic drive, smaller, lower-threshold motor neurons are usually recruited before larger ones.
  • For a comparable current, smaller neurons with higher input resistance undergo a larger excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP). In V = I × R, V is voltage change, I is current, and R is resistance.
  • Intrinsic motor-neuron properties and spinal circuitry help set the usual order, while descending commands provide task-related drive. Fatigue-resistant units are generally recruited early.
  • Motor units need not fire synchronously; distributing activity can help sustain force and delay fatigue rather than eliminating it.

4 ways to increase force

WayKey point
RecruitmentActivates additional motor units. The lecture emphasizes its contribution in large muscles such as those of the legs; its relative importance depends on the muscle and task.
Firing rateHigher discharge frequency allows successive twitches to summate, potentially producing a fused tetanic contraction. The lecture emphasizes rate coding in hand muscles; both recruitment and rate coding contribute to normal force control.
Firing patternA brief interval between successive impulses can enhance twitch summation, provided the muscle has not fully relaxed between them.
Length–tensionActive tension is greatest near optimal actin–myosin overlap (a sarcomere length of approximately 2.0–2.3 micrometres, µm, in the lecture). Excessive shortening causes filament interference; excessive lengthening reduces overlap. Passive elastic tension rises with stretch, so total tension is not the same as active tension.

Neuromuscular junction (NMJ)

  • A large, specialized synapse with postsynaptic junctional folds and dense nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs). The lecture contrasts approximate contact areas of 1000 and 0.05 square micrometres (µm²) for a muscle endplate and a central synapse; these are illustrative, not fixed sizes for all synapses.
  • In a healthy NMJ, one motor-neuron action potential (AP) normally releases enough acetylcholine (ACh) to bring the endplate to threshold and trigger a muscle AP. This safety factor avoids a need for temporal summation at the endplate; transmission can fail in disease or with drugs.
  • Receptor opening produces net cation entry, particularly sodium (Na⁺). Voltage-gated channels then propagate the muscle AP; potassium (K⁺) currents contribute to repolarization.

Lecture 2

Structure & Contraction

Sarcomere: Z disk to Z disk

  • A (anisotropic) band: spans the thick-filament length, including overlap with thin filaments; its length stays essentially constant during shortening.
  • I (isotropic) band: contains thin filaments without thick-filament overlap; a Z disk bisects it.
  • H zone: central thick-filament-only region; the M line lies at its centre.
  • During shortening, the I band and H zone narrow; the A band does not shorten because the filaments slide rather than become shorter.

Contractile and supporting proteins

  • Myosin forms thick filaments; its heads have actin-binding sites and adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) activity, which hydrolyzes adenosine triphosphate (ATP).
  • Actin forms the thin-filament backbone and bears myosin-binding sites.
  • Tropomyosin limits access to those sites at rest.
  • Troponin T binds tropomyosin; troponin I is inhibitory; troponin C (TnC) binds calcium ions (Ca²⁺).
  • Titin is an elastic protein spanning from the Z disk toward the M line; nebulin helps organize and stabilize thin filaments.

Excitation–contraction coupling

In skeletal muscle, the dihydropyridine (DHP) receptor in the transverse (T) tubule acts as a voltage sensor coupled to ryanodine receptor type 1 (RyR1) in the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR).

AP along sarcolemma and T tubule→DHP receptor changes conformation→RyR1 opens: Ca²⁺ leaves SR→Ca²⁺ binds TnC→Tropomyosin moves→Cross-bridge cycling
Skeletal-muscle distinction: DHP receptor voltage sensing and mechanical coupling to RyR1 are central here; do not substitute the cardiac-muscle model of calcium-induced calcium release.

Cross-bridge cycle

ATP binding detaches myosin from actin→ATP hydrolysis energizes the myosin head→Head binds an exposed actin site→Phosphate release and power stroke→ADP release; next ATP allows detachment

ADP means adenosine diphosphate; Pi means inorganic phosphate. Calcium exposes binding sites through troponin/tropomyosin; it does not directly provide the energy for the stroke.

Relaxation

  • Motor-neuron firing ceases and AChE clears ACh from the synaptic cleft.
  • Sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA) pumps calcium back into the SR; calsequestrin binds calcium within the SR.
  • As cytosolic calcium falls, calcium leaves TnC and tropomyosin again restricts actin–myosin binding.
  • Elastic recoil and opposing muscle forces can restore length; calcium removal and length restoration are different processes.

Roles of ATP

  • ATP binding detaches myosin; hydrolysis energizes the head for a subsequent stroke.
  • SERCA consumes ATP to reuptake calcium.
  • The sodium–potassium ATPase (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase) maintains ion gradients over time. Rapid AP repolarization depends mainly on ion-channel currents, not directly on a pump cycle.
Rigor mortis: after death, ATP depletion prevents normal calcium reuptake and actin–myosin detachment. Stiffness later resolves through protein breakdown. The lecture’s onset of about 3–4 hours (h) and peak near 12 h are approximate; timing varies with conditions and is not a precise postmortem clock.
Lecture 3

Touch & Muscle Receptors

Muscle spindle

  • Senses muscle length (proprioception)
  • Intrafusal fibres have sensory endings around their central region; gamma (γ) motor neurons activate their contractile ends.
  • Group Ia primary afferents: signal length and the rate of length change (dynamic response).
  • Group II secondary afferents: predominantly signal maintained, static muscle length.
  • Stretch-sensitive channels: more stretch, more depolarisation

Alpha–gamma (α–γ) co-activation

  • Alpha (α) motor neurons activate extrafusal fibres. Shortening without matching gamma activity can unload the spindle and reduce its sensory discharge.
  • Concurrent gamma activity shortens the intrafusal contractile ends, helping keep the sensory region taut and responsive.
  • Static gamma activity emphasizes maintained-length sensitivity; dynamic gamma activity emphasizes sensitivity to the speed of stretch.

Conduction categories: fine touch and proprioception commonly use large myelinated fibres. A-alpha (Aα) and A-beta (Aβ) are conduction-based labels; the Ia/Ib/II group labels describe sensory-afferent categories. These are related classification systems, not interchangeable names for a single receptor type.

Four touch mechanoreceptors

Rapidly adapting (RA) receptors respond particularly to changes in stimulation; slowly adapting (SA) receptors maintain discharge during sustained stimulation. Type 1 receptors generally have smaller receptive fields than type 2 receptors. Thus RA1 means rapidly adapting type 1, and SA2 means slowly adapting type 2.

Coding

  • Rate coding: stimulus intensity is represented partly by the frequency of action potentials per second, not by increasing the size of individual spikes.
  • RA responses emphasize onset, offset, or other changes; SA responses persist during maintained stimulation.
  • For SA1 responses, interpret probe size together with force, indentation, and receptor position. “The smallest probe always gives the highest rate” is not a general rule independent of experimental conditions.
  • Stimulus location depends on which receptors and pathways are active and their receptive fields.

Acuity & pathways

  • Small receptive fields and dense innervation support fine two-point discrimination, especially at the fingertips, lips, and tongue.
  • For the trunk and limbs, primary sensory-neuron cell bodies lie in dorsal root ganglia and enter the spinal cord through dorsal roots. Do not apply this spinal description unchanged to facial sensation.
  • A dermatome is a skin region supplied predominantly by one spinal nerve root; neighbouring dermatomes overlap.
  • The sensory homunculus is a somatotopic map in the postcentral gyrus. Cortical representation reflects sensory processing demands and innervation, not physical body-part size; the body is represented predominantly on the opposite side.
Last look

High-Yield Summary

  • Size principle: smaller, lower-threshold units are generally recruited first. Both recruitment and firing rate contribute to force control; their proportions vary by muscle and task.
  • Healthy NMJ transmission has a safety factor: one nerve AP normally triggers a muscle AP. Myasthenia gravis affects postsynaptic transmission; botulinum toxin reduces release; organophosphates inhibit AChE.
  • Skeletal-muscle coupling: DHP voltage sensing → RyR1 opening → calcium release → troponin C binding → tropomyosin movement; A-band length stays constant.
  • ATP permits myosin detachment and energizing, calcium reuptake by SERCA, and maintenance of ion gradients. ATP depletion contributes to rigor.
  • Ia = length and rate of change; II = mainly static length; Ib = tendon tension. Alpha–gamma co-activation maintains spindle responsiveness during shortening.
  • Meissner/Merkel: superficial and small-field; Pacinian: vibration; Ruffini: skin stretch. RA means rapidly adapting here, not rheumatoid arthritis.
