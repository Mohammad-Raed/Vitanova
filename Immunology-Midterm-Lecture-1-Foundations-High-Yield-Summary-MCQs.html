Why it matters

Roles of the immune system

1. Defence against infection

Protects the host from viruses, bacteria, fungi and parasites by recognising them and triggering responses that eliminate them.

2. Immunosurveillance

Recognises and removes tumour cells. Some tumours develop immune evasion mechanisms to escape.

3. Responses to grafts and new proteins

Foreign graft components are recognised as non-self → transplant rejection. Anti-drug antibodies can form against biologic drugs and reduce their efficacy.

4. Broad recognition

Antibodies can bind almost any molecule: proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, nucleic acids and small chemicals (haptens). Haptens usually must be attached to a carrier protein to induce a full response.

5. Tissue maintenance

Tissue homeostasis: clears dead cells and debris and supports repair, e.g. macrophages remove dead cells by phagocytosis.

6. When defence turns on self

Failure of self-tolerance → autoimmune disease (type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, SLE). About 10% of people live with at least one autoimmune disease (Conrad et al., Lancet 2023).
Core vocabulary

Key definitions

Immunity

Resistance to disease, e.g. HBV vaccine → protective anti-HBs.

Immunology

Study of host responses to foreign (and self) substances.

Antigen

Any molecule recognised by an antibody or T-cell receptor.

Immunogen

An antigen that can induce an immune response. Not every antigen is an immunogen (haptens need a carrier).

Epitope

Antigenic determinant: the small part of an antigen bound by one antibody.

Antibody

Immunoglobulin: a glycoprotein secreted by plasma cells that recognises specific targets.

Serology

Antigen–antibody testing of serum (e.g. HBsAg, anti-HIV), used in diagnostic tests.

Vaccination

Deliberate exposure to antigen to induce protective immunity, so the body is ready for a future encounter.
The big picture

How the immune system is organised

Immune defence→Innate (natural): non-specific · fast · no memory+Adaptive (acquired): specific · slower · memory
  • Innate components: epithelial barriers, phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages), dendritic cells, NK cells, complement, inflammation.
  • Adaptive arms: Humoral (B cells → antibodies) and Cell-mediated (T lymphocytes).
  • How adaptive immunity is acquired: Active (infection, vaccination) or Passive (transferred antibodies).
Innate and adaptive immunity work together: innate cells detect danger first and then instruct lymphocytes what to respond to and how.
First line of defence

Innate (natural) immunity

  • Always present in healthy individuals, ready before infection.
  • Rapid: acts within minutes to hours.
  • Recognises shared patterns: PAMPs on microbes and DAMPs from damaged cells, through pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs).
  • Main components:
    • Epithelial barriers and antimicrobial peptides
    • Phagocytes: neutrophils and macrophages
    • Dendritic cells, NK cells, innate lymphoid cells
    • Complement proteins and inflammation mediators (TNF, IL-1, IL-6)
  • No antigen-specific memory, although trained immunity shows innate cells can be reprogrammed to respond more efficiently on later encounters.

Illustrated in the lecture: epithelial barrier with antimicrobial peptides; macrophages whose TLRs sense PAMPs; neutrophils; NK cells killing infected cells; complement coating (opsonising) microbes.

The splinter story (innate immunity in action)

1 Splinter carries bacteria in (0 min)→2 Macrophages sense microbes, release cytokines (minutes)→3 Redness, heat, swelling, pain: vasodilation and leaky vessels→4 Neutrophils arrive in numbers, engulf bacteria (hours) → pus → resolution
  • Non-specific: same response to any bacterium that enters.
  • Rapid: starts within minutes; inflammation within hours.
  • No memory: a second splinter triggers the same response, with the same speed and no improvement.
Second line of defence

Adaptive (acquired) immunity

  • Stimulated by exposure to microbes or antigens that enter tissues.
  • Mediated by lymphocytes (B cells and T cells) and their products.
  • Specific: distinguishes closely related molecules.
  • Slower on first exposure: about 5–7 days to peak.
  • Memory: protection results from prior experience with the antigen.
The Edward Jenner story (1796): material from cowpox lesions protected a boy (James Phipps) against smallpox. This was the first vaccine (vacca = cow). It illustrates antigen specificity, a slower first response and long-lived memory.

The bridge

Dendritic cells link innate and adaptive immunity

  • Dendritic cells capture antigen in tissues and migrate through lymphatics to lymph nodes.
  • After engulfing and digesting the microbe, they cut it into peptides and display them on MHC (HLA) molecules.
  • MHC class I → CD8⁺ T cells (cytotoxic). MHC class II → CD4⁺ T cells (helper).

Naïve T cells need three signals

Signal 1: TCR binds peptide–MHC (specificity)+Signal 2: co-stimulation (B7 on DC – CD28 on T cell); prevents random activation+Signal 3: cytokines direct the type of response
Clinical link: HLA matching is essential in transplantation to prevent rejection. Checkpoint inhibitors (modern cancer drugs) act on these signals to unleash anti-tumour T cells.
Adaptive arm 1

Humoral immunity

  • Mediated by antibodies produced by B lymphocytes → plasma cells.
  • Secreted into blood and mucosal fluids.
  • Targets extracellular microbes and microbial toxins.
  • Antibody functions: neutralisation of viruses and toxins; opsonisation for phagocytosis; complement activation; ADCC (antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity) by NK cells.

Adaptive arm 2

Cell-mediated immunity

  • Mediated by T lymphocytes; does not act through antibodies.
  • Targets: intracellular microbes (viruses, Mycobacterium tuberculosis) and tumour cells.
  • CD4⁺ helper T cells secrete cytokines: Th1 activate macrophages (IFN-γ helps kill ingested microbes) · Th2 → eosinophils · Th17 → neutrophils · Tfh help B cells make high-affinity antibodies.
  • CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells kill infected or tumour cells with perforin and granzymes.
  • Regulatory T cells (Treg) restrain responses and protect self.
Clinical clue: low CD4⁺ counts in HIV lead to opportunistic infections by intracellular organisms, because effective cell-mediated immunity is lacking.

Cardinal features

Specificity, memory and tolerance

1 · Specificity and diversity: clonal selection

  • Diversity: ~10⁷–10⁹ distinct lymphocyte clones recognise a vast range of antigens.
  • Receptors are formed before antigen exposure by V(D)J gene recombination.
  • Specificity: each clone carries receptors for one epitope.
  • Clonal selection: antigen binds and activates only the matching clone.
  • Clonal expansion → effector cells (clear antigen) + memory cells (persist).
Clinical link: RAG gene defects → SCID. CAR-T cells are given engineered specificity against cancer.

2 · Immunological memory

Primary response (first exposure)Secondary / anamnestic (re-exposure)
CellsNaïve lymphocytesMemory lymphocytes
Lag5–10 days1–3 days
AntibodyMainly IgM; modest peakMainly IgG; higher affinity
Size/durationSmaller, shorter10–100× greater and longer lasting
Clinical use: booster doses; IgM vs IgG serology distinguishes recent from past infection.

3 · Self / non-self recognition: tolerance

  • The immune system normally does not attack the host: self-tolerance.
  • Central tolerance: strongly self-reactive lymphocytes are deleted in the thymus (T) and bone marrow (B).
  • Peripheral tolerance: anergy, deletion and suppression by regulatory T cells (FOXP3⁺ Tregs).
  • Failure of tolerance → autoimmune disease: type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, multiple sclerosis.
  • Single-gene lessons: AIRE defect → APS-1; FOXP3 defect → IPEX.
Nobel Prize 2025 (Physiology or Medicine): Brunkow, Ramsdell and Sakaguchi, for discoveries on peripheral immune tolerance (regulatory T cells).

Summary

High-yield take-home messages

  1. Immunity = resistance to disease; antibodies and T cells recognise epitopes on antigens.
  2. Innate: fast, non-specific, no memory. Adaptive: specific, slower (5–7 days), remembers.
  3. Humoral (B cells, antibodies) clears extracellular microbes; cell-mediated (T cells) handles intracellular microbes and tumours.
  4. Active immunity gives lasting memory; passive gives immediate, temporary protection.
  5. Specificity, diversity, memory and self-tolerance define adaptive immunity.
  6. Failure → hypersensitivity, autoimmunity, immunodeficiency or immunoproliferative disease.

Quick-recall

  • Antigen presentation: MHC I → CD8⁺; MHC II → CD4⁺. Three signals: TCR–peptide/MHC, B7–CD28, cytokines.
  • Isotypes: IgM first · IgG main, crosses placenta · IgA mucosa/milk · IgE allergy/helminths · IgD naïve B-cell receptor.
  • Th1 → macrophages · Th2 → eosinophils · Th17 → neutrophils · Tfh → B cells · CD8⁺ kill via perforin/granzymes · Treg suppress (FOXP3).
  • Primary: lag 5–10 d, IgM. Secondary: lag 1–3 d, IgG, 10–100× greater.
  • RAG → SCID · AIRE → APS-1 · FOXP3 → IPEX · low CD4⁺ in HIV → opportunistic infection.
