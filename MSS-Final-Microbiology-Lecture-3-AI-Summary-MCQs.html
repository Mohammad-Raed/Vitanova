Fungal basics

Basics

Three fungal forms

Spot it: Mould at 25–30 °C, yeast at 37 °C = dimorphic fungus.

  • Yeasts: unicellular, oval or round; most reproduce asexually by budding.
  • Moulds: multicellular and filamentous. Branching hyphae form a mycelium.
  • Dimorphic fungi: switch between mould and yeast depending on conditions, mainly temperature.

Mnemonic: “Mould in the cold, yeast in the heat.”

Basics

Mould vs yeast

Spot it: Fuzzy or cottony colony with hyphae = mould; smooth creamy colony like bacteria = yeast.

Basics

Classification of mycoses

Spot it: Depth of tissue involved decides the group.

  1. Superficial: skin, hair, nails and mucous membranes. The most common fungal infections, found worldwide.
  2. Subcutaneous: dermis, subcutaneous tissue and nearby bone. Mainly tropics and subtropics. Follow traumatic inoculation of soil or plant fungi.
  3. Systemic: deep-seated infections.

Superficial mycoses

Superficial

Superficial mycoses: overview

Spot it: Minimal tissue damage and no inflammatory response.

Four diseases: piedra, tinea (pityriasis) versicolor, dermatophytosis and superficial candidiasis.

Superficial

Piedra

Spot it: Nodules stuck on the hair shaft; treat by shaving.

  • Fungal infection of the hair shaft with nodular growth.
  • White piedra: cream nodules, easily removed.
  • Black piedra: black nodules, firmly adherent.
  • Treatment: mainly shaving the infected hair.
Superficial

Tinea (pityriasis) versicolor

Spot it: Hyper- or hypopigmented macules on upper trunk and back, caused by a lipid-loving yeast.

  • Cause: Malassezia furfur, a lipophilic yeast that is part of normal skin flora.
  • Features: hyper- or hypopigmented macules, mostly on the upper trunk and back. Usually asymptomatic.
  • Treatment: selenium sulfide or topical azoles.

Dermatophytosis (ringworm)

Dermatophyte

Dermatophytes: basics and diagnosis

Spot it: KOH shows hyphae; culture takes 7–28 days.

  • Infect the keratinised tissue: stratum corneum, hair and nails. Also called ringworm or tinea, named by the body site.
  • Spread by direct or indirect contact with an infected person, animal or soil. Dermatophytosis is the truly contagious superficial mycosis.

By natural habitat

  • Anthropophilic (humans): T. tonsurans
  • Zoophilic (animals): M. canis (cats, dogs), T. verrucosum (cattle)
  • Geophilic (soil): M. gypseum

By genus

  • Microsporum: skin and hair
  • Trichophyton: skin, hair and nails
  • Epidermophyton: skin and nails

Lab diagnosis

Skin scrapings, plucked hair or nail clippings. KOH preparation shows fungal hyphae. Culture on mycological media takes 7–28 days.

Mnemonic: “Epidermophyton spares hair; Microsporum spares nails.”

Dermatophyte

Clinical features and treatment

Spot it: Ring with central clearing = skin; scaly patch of hair loss = scalp; thick, crumbly nail = onychomycosis.

  • Skin: red, scaly patches that spread outward with central clearing.
  • Hair-bearing areas: raised, red, scaly, ring-shaped patch of alopecia.
  • Nails (onychomycosis): thickened, discoloured, raised and friable.

Treatment

  • Skin: topical azoles or terbinafine for 2–6 weeks.
  • Other sites: oral azoles or terbinafine.
  • Duration: hair-bearing areas 6–8 weeks, fingernails up to 4 months, toenails 6–12 months.

Skin candidiasis

Candida

Superficial (cutaneous) candidiasis

Spot it: Moist skin folds, itchy red rash with satellite pustules, in a diabetic or obese patient = candidal intertrigo.

  • Both a superficial and an opportunistic mycosis: affects immunocompetent and immunocompromised people.
  • Candida albicans causes 80–90% of cases.
  • Candidal intertrigo: occluded, moist skin folds (axilla, groin, under breasts, toe webs, nappy area). Common with obesity and diabetes. Itchy red rash with vesiculopustular lesions.
  • Nail and nearby skin: onychomycosis and chronic paronychia.
Immunocompromised

Candidiasis in immunocompromised patients

Spot it: Child with severe, recurrent mucocutaneous Candida and a T-cell defect = CMC; scattered red papules in a septic, immunosuppressed patient = macronodular candidiasis.

  • Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (CMC): children and young adults. Severe, persistent infection caused by a genetic T-cell defect against C. albicans. Can disfigure the face, scalp and hands and cause oesophageal stenosis.
  • Macronodular candidiasis: skin sign of candidaemia (bloodstream spread). Several red papules and pustules.

Subcutaneous mycoses

Subcutaneous

Subcutaneous mycoses: overview

Spot it: Trauma plus soil or plant material, then a chronic abscess, ulcer or sinus.

  • Caused by traumatic inoculation into deeper tissue.
  • Form abscesses, non-healing ulcers and draining sinuses.
  • Mostly chronic; rarely spread systemically.
  • Three diseases: sporotrichosis, mycetoma, chromoblastomycosis.
Subcutaneous

Sporotrichosis (rose gardener’s disease)

Spot it: Gardener pricked by a rose thorn, then nodules along the lymphatics: Sporothrix schenckii.

  • Cause: dimorphic fungus Sporothrix schenckii, found in soil, plants and organic matter.
  • Entry: recreational or occupational inoculation (e.g. rose thorns).
  • Course: nodule at the inoculation site that may ulcerate. About 2 weeks later, new nodules along the draining lymphatics, which may also ulcerate.
Subcutaneous

Sporotrichosis: mould and yeast phases

Spot it: Cigar-shaped yeast at 37 °C; mould with spores at 25–30 °C. Treat with potassium iodide or itraconazole.

  • Mould with septate hyphae in nature and in culture at 25–30 °C.
  • Yeast (round or cigar-shaped cells) in tissue and in culture at 37 °C.
  • Diagnosis: culture of pus or infected tissue.
  • Treatment: oral potassium iodide or itraconazole.
Subcutaneous

Mycetoma (Madura foot)

Spot it: Swollen foot with multiple draining sinuses and grains in the pus.

  • What: chronic granulomatous infection of skin, subcutaneous tissue, fascia and bone. Most often the foot or hand. Tropics and subtropics.
  • Causes: actinomycetes (actinomycetoma) or moulds (eumycetoma). The most common eumycetoma agent is Madurella mycetomatis.
  • Entry: thorns or wood splinters, typically in agricultural workers.
  • Grains: compact colonies of the organism, 0.5–2 mm, coloured according to the species.
  • Diagnosis: grains in pus from draining sinuses or in biopsy.
  • Treatment: in eumycetoma antifungals work poorly, so radical surgery is usually needed.

Triad: swelling + draining sinuses + grains.

Subcutaneous

Chromoblastomycosis (chromomycosis)

Spot it: Warty (verrucous) limb lesions, tropical, Fonsecaea pedrosoi.

  • What: chronic, localised infection of skin and subcutaneous tissue. Slow-growing verrucous (wart-like) lesions, usually on the limbs.
  • Commonest cause: Fonsecaea pedrosoi (also Cladophialophora), mainly in the tropics.
  • Treatment: long courses of itraconazole or terbinafine. Surgical excision for some localised lesions.
Subcutaneous

Chromoblastomycosis: lab diagnosis

Spot it: Brown, thick-walled sclerotic (Medlar) bodies = copper penny bodies.

  • Microscopy: sclerotic (Medlar) bodies, also called copper penny bodies: thick-walled, brown, round cells.
  • Culture: slow-growing dark (dematiaceous) colonies.

High-yield summary

  • Dimorphic: mould at 25–30 °C, yeast at 37 °C.
  • Only dermatophytosis is truly contagious among superficial mycoses.
  • Piedra: hair-shaft nodules (white = soft, black = hard). Treat by shaving.
  • Tinea versicolor: Malassezia furfur; selenium sulfide or azoles.
  • Dermatophyte genera: Microsporum (skin, hair), Trichophyton (skin, hair, nails), Epidermophyton (skin, nails).
  • KOH for hyphae; culture takes 7–28 days.
  • Candida albicans = 80–90% of skin candidiasis; intertrigo in obesity and diabetes.
  • CMC = T-cell defect; macronodular = candidaemia.
  • Sporotrichosis: rose thorn, lymphatic nodules, cigar-shaped yeast; KI or itraconazole.
  • Mycetoma: swelling, sinuses, grains; Madurella mycetomatis; surgery.
  • Chromoblastomycosis: verrucous lesions; Fonsecaea pedrosoi; copper penny bodies.
