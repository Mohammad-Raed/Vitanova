Lecture 1

Skin Pharmacology: Psoriasis

Disease basics

  • Chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease with an important T-lymphocyte component; plaque psoriasis typically produces well-demarcated red plaques with silvery scales.
  • The lecture cites prevalence estimates of approximately 3% in White populations and 1% in African populations. These are population estimates, not fixed individual risks; prevalence varies by population and study.
  • Plaque psoriasis (psoriasis vulgaris) accounts for approximately 80% of cases in the lecture.
  • Diagnosis is usually clinical; there is no single diagnostic blood test. A skin biopsy can help when the appearance is atypical.

Psoriasis and rheumatoid arthritis (RA)

  • Psoriasis is skin-predominant but has systemic inflammatory associations. Its immune biology is not limited to T cells alone.
  • RA is systemic autoimmune inflammatory arthritis involving T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, and other immune cells.
  • Psoriatic arthritis can accompany psoriasis and is not restricted to severe skin disease. Inflammatory mediators and matrix metalloproteinases contribute to joint damage.

Associations

  • Separate disease associations from treatment-related risks; do not interpret the lecture shorthand as proof that untreated psoriasis itself causes skin cancer.
  • Assess cardiometabolic comorbidities: hyperlipidemia, hyperglycemia, and cardiovascular disease (CVD). These are associated conditions, not all interchangeable diagnostic criteria for metabolic syndrome.
  • Possible flare triggers include stress, smoking, and beta-adrenoceptor blockers (β-blockers); an association does not mean that every exposed patient will flare.

Pathophysiology

Interleukins (ILs), including IL-17, IL-22, and IL-23, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) participate in an interacting inflammatory network.

Trigger→Dendritic-cell signaling→Pathogenic T-cell responses→IL-23/IL-17 signaling and TNF-α→Keratinocyte proliferation and inflammation

Management principles

  • Measures include stress reduction, moisturizers, and supervised ultraviolet (UV) phototherapy.
  • Topical treatment is commonly used for limited disease; systemic treatment, which may be oral or injected, is considered according to disease burden, affected sites, and joint involvement. Topicals can still cause systemic adverse effects if sufficiently absorbed.
  • Ointments are generally more occlusive than creams or lotions; absorption also depends on the drug, body site, inflammation, and formulation.

Lecture 2

Anti-Rheumatoid Drugs

  • Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory disease. Treatment aims for remission or low disease activity, prevention of joint damage, and preservation of function.
  • Pathogenesis involves antigen presentation, cluster of differentiation 4–positive (CD4⁺) T cells, cytokines such as IL-17, TNF-α, IL-15, and IL-18, and B-cell/plasma-cell autoantibodies. Receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa B ligand (RANKL) promotes osteoclast-mediated bone erosion; cartilage loss has additional inflammatory mechanisms.
  • Management combines medication with appropriate joint protection, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and surgery when indicated.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)

Relieve pain and inflammation but do not prevent structural joint damage. Selective cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibition may reduce some gastrointestinal tract (GIT) adverse effects compared with nonselective inhibition; it does not remove gastrointestinal, renal, or cardiovascular risks. Analgesia can begin within hours; anti-inflammatory benefit may take longer.

Corticosteroids

Can provide rapid symptom control as bridge therapy while disease-modifying drugs take effect. Oral and intra-articular routes have different exposure and safety considerations; bridge therapy does not imply indefinite treatment.

Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs)

Disease-modifying treatment aims to limit inflammatory damage, unlike NSAIDs used alone. DMARDs include conventional synthetic drugs, biologics, and targeted synthetic drugs. Early assessment and treatment are important; established X-ray damage is not a prerequisite.

Lecture 3

Muscle Relaxants

  • Skeletal muscle is controlled by somatic motor neurons. At the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), acetylcholine (ACh) activates muscle-type nicotinic receptors. Net cation entry, particularly sodium (Na⁺), depolarizes the endplate; the resulting muscle action potential leads to calcium (Ca²⁺) release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) and contraction.
  • Neuromuscular blockers act at the NMJ. Many spasmolytics act in the central nervous system (CNS); dantrolene instead acts directly on skeletal-muscle calcium release.

Nondepolarizing (antagonists)

  • Cisatracurium, pancuronium, rocuronium, vecuronium (replaced tubocurarine).
  • Competitively block nicotinic receptors without stimulating them.
  • Usually given intravenously (IV); intramuscular (IM) administration is agent-specific. Their charged structure gives poor oral absorption and little penetration of the blood–brain barrier (BBB).
Face/eyes→Fingers/limbs/neck/trunk→Intercostals→Diaphragm
The lecture describes recovery in roughly reverse order; individual muscles and agents need not follow an identical sequence. Paralysis does not itself provide unconsciousness or pain relief.

Depolarizing: succinylcholine

  • The depolarizing blocker discussed here. It is not rapidly broken down by synaptic acetylcholinesterase, but is normally hydrolyzed by plasma butyrylcholinesterase; “resists cholinesterase” without this distinction is misleading.
  • Phase I: sustained endplate depolarization inactivates nearby voltage-gated sodium channels. Initial fasciculations are followed by paralysis; paralysis does not require phase II.
  • Phase II: after prolonged exposure, the membrane may repolarize while transmission remains impaired through desensitization and related changes.
  • Rapid onset explains its use in rapid airway management; suitability depends on contraindications and clinical context.
  • Adverse effects (AEs) include apnea from respiratory-muscle paralysis, hyperkalemia (raised plasma potassium, K⁺), and triggering of malignant hyperthermia in susceptible people. Dantrolene reduces SR calcium release and is a specific treatment for malignant hyperthermia, not for every fever.

Orphenadrine and baclofen therefore have different indications and are not interchangeable merely because both are called muscle relaxants.
