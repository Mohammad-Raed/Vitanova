1. Assessing lymphadenopathy

Lymphadenopathy is enlargement or abnormality of lymph nodes.

  • Ask first: localised or generalised?
  • Localised: search the drainage territory for (1) infection, (2) injury or (3) tumour.
  • Generalised: EBV (Epstein–Barr virus), CMV (cytomegalovirus), toxoplasmosis (a parasitic infection), HIV, TB, lymphoma, SLE (systemic lupus erythematosus, an autoimmune disease), drug reactions.
  • Examine: site, size, consistency, tenderness, mobility, overlying skin; check liver and spleen.
  • Reassuring: small, soft, tender, mobile, resolving within a few weeks.
  • Investigate: full blood count and film, ESR/CRP, serology, chest imaging, ultrasound.
  • Biopsy: excisional biopsy when lymphoma is suspected, because it preserves node architecture.
LecturerAn ordinary infection usually causes swelling that returns to normal size. If the features differ from this pattern (see red flags), the risk of cancer is higher.
ClarificationSerology looks for antibodies formed against agents such as CMV and EBV. Ultrasound shows the shape of the node. The lymphoma diagnosis depends on knowing the arrangement of cells and their relationships, hence excisional biopsy (not fine-needle aspiration) to retain the architecture.

Red flags (memorise)

  • ✔ >2 cm or growing
  • ✔ Hard, fixed, painless
  • ✔ Supraclavicular site (Virchow node)
  • ✔ Fever, night sweats, weight loss
  • ✔ No infective cause found → excisional biopsy if lymphoma is suspected

2. The spleen: the blood filter

  • Largest secondary lymphoid organ: about 150 g, left upper quadrant, under ribs 9–11.
  • Filter for blood: no afferent lymphatics, so antigen arrives in the bloodstream.
  • Removes aged and damaged red cells and "pits" inclusions from otherwise healthy cells.
  • Recycles iron from haemoglobin breakdown.
  • Stores platelets: roughly one third of the platelet mass.
  • Mounts responses to blood-borne antigens, especially encapsulated bacteria (marked important).
  • Filters ~5% of cardiac output.
  • Clinical: normally impalpable; splenomegaly occurs in portal hypertension, haemolysis, infection (malaria, EBV) or haematological malignancy.
ClarificationPitting: the spleen removes inclusions from RBCs without destroying the cell.
ClarificationRemoval of old RBCs: RBCs live about 120 days, after which they become less deformable. They cannot squeeze through the narrow passages of the splenic red pulp, where macrophages trap and break them down.
ClarificationComparison with the kidney: the renal glomerulus also filters blood, but it filters water and small solutes from plasma, and its main purpose is urine formation. The spleen's filtering is of cells and particles for immune and clearance purposes.

4. Asplenia and hyposplenism

Asplenia is absence of the spleen; hyposplenism is a spleen that is present but functions poorly.

  • Causes: splenectomy after trauma, sickle-cell autosplenectomy, coeliac disease, infiltration.
  • Blood film clue: Howell–Jolly bodies (nuclear remnants no longer pitted out), target cells, thrombocytosis. These indicate loss of splenic function.
  • Key risk: overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI), rapid sepsis with encapsulated bacteria. This is an expected consequence of losing the spleen.
  • Organisms (important): Streptococcus pneumoniae (most common), Haemophilus influenzae type b, meningococcus, Capnocytophaga after dog bites; severe malaria and babesiosis.
  • Prevention: vaccinate (pneumococcal, Hib, MenACWY and MenB, annual influenza); antibiotic prophylaxis, especially in children and the first 2 years; alert card, emergency antibiotic supply, urgent review if febrile, travel/malaria advice.
ClarificationAlert card: an asplenic or hyposplenic patient carries a card or bracelet stating this.
ClarificationCoeliac disease: an immune response against gluten (found in wheat and barley) damages the intestinal villi.
ClarificationIn hyposplenism the spleen is present but its function is impaired. The note on the slide states that the main idea is now clear and the remaining details are for memorisation.
LecturerA separate remark linked antibiotic use to Clostridium difficile becoming the dominant gut bacterium and causing infection.
NoteThis remark is not part of coeliac disease, which is gluten-triggered; antibiotic-associated C. difficile infection is a separate condition.

5. MALT: guarding the mucosal surfaces

  • Largest lymphoid compartment: more lymphocytes than in all other organs combined.
  • Sites: gut (Peyer's patches, appendix, isolated follicles, mesenteric nodes), Waldeyer's ring (tonsils and adenoids), bronchial (BALT) and urogenital tissue. The sites marked on the slide are places where IgA is released.
  • M cells: specialised epithelial cells that sample luminal antigen and deliver it to dendritic cells.
  • Organised like a node: follicles with germinal centres and adjacent T zones, but no capsule and no afferent lymphatics.
  • Default setting is tolerance to food and commensal bacteria; failure → coeliac disease, IBD.
  • Main product is secretory IgA: 3–5 g secreted daily.
  • Vaccines: oral rotavirus, polio, typhoid and cholera vaccines target this tissue.
ClarificationThe M cell takes up the antigen and passes it to the dendritic cell, an APC, which initiates activation of the response that leads to IgA production.
ClarificationThese vaccines target pathogens that affect the digestive tract.

6. Secretory IgA

Secretory IgA (sIgA) is the primary antibody responsible for protecting mucosal surfaces.

  1. Made as a dimer: two IgA molecules joined by a J chain, produced by plasma cells in the lamina propria.
  2. Transported by pIgR (polymeric immunoglobulin receptor), which carries it across the epithelial cell (transcytosis).
  3. Secretory component: the part of pIgR that stays attached and protects IgA from digestion by enzymes in mucus.
  4. Function: immune exclusion. Binds microbes and toxins, blocks attachment to mucosal surfaces, and does so without triggering inflammation.
  5. Also in breast milk: passive mucosal protection for the newborn (Lecture 1).

Clinical: selective IgA deficiency is the most common primary immunodeficiency (~1 in 300–700). It is usually mild. Total IgA must be checked when interpreting coeliac serology (IgA-based tests can be falsely negative), and there is a risk of transfusion reactions to blood products.

7. Skin-associated lymphoid tissue (SALT)

  • Keratinocytes are active: they make antimicrobial peptides (defensins) and cytokines when injured.
  • Langerhans cells: dendritic cells within the epidermis that carry antigen to the draining node.
  • Dermal dendritic cells and macrophages sample the deeper layers.
  • Resident memory T cells (TRM) stay in skin long-term and respond at once to a repeat exposure.
  • Mast cells sit around dermal vessels, ready for immediate hypersensitivity.
  • Skin homing: T cells returning to skin carry cutaneous lymphocyte antigen (CLA) (with CCR4).
  • Clinical: contact dermatitis is a T-cell (type IV) reaction; psoriasis is driven by the IL-23/Th17 axis.

8. When lymphatics fail or are exploited

ConditionKey points
LymphoedemaProtein-rich swelling when drainage fails. Secondary: surgery, radiotherapy, tumour, and filariasis (commonest cause worldwide). Primary: congenital malformation (Milroy disease). Treated with compression, skin care and exercise; microsurgery in selected cases.
Sentinel node biopsyThe first node draining a tumour is mapped with dye or tracer and examined; standard in melanoma and breast cancer.
LymphangitisTender red streak tracking from an infected wound.
MetastasisCarcinomas commonly spread first to regional nodes; node status therefore drives cancer staging.

9. Research in 2026: new views of the lymphatic system

  • Tertiary lymphoid structures: organised B and T aggregates in chronically inflamed tissue and inside tumours; their presence predicts response to checkpoint immunotherapy.
  • Meningeal lymphatics: the brain does have lymphatic drainage; vessels alongside the dural sinuses carry fluid and immune cells to deep cervical nodes. Studied in Alzheimer's disease and brain tumours.
  • Node-targeted vaccines: formulations (lipid nanoparticles, albumin-binding vaccines) designed to drain into the node improve responses.
  • Imaging: PET-CT and contrast lymphography map node involvement for staging.

High-yield revision summary — Lecture 8

  • Lymphadenopathy: localised → check drainage area (infection, injury, tumour); generalised → EBV, CMV, toxoplasmosis, HIV, TB, lymphoma, SLE, drugs. Reassuring: small, soft, tender, mobile, resolves in weeks.
  • Red flags: >2 cm/growing, hard-fixed-painless, supraclavicular, B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss), no infective cause. Excisional biopsy preserves architecture (lymphoma).
  • Spleen: ~150 g, LUQ, ribs 9–11; no afferent lymphatics; clears aged RBCs (~120 days, less deformable), pits inclusions, recycles iron, stores ~1/3 platelets, responds to blood-borne antigens (encapsulated bacteria).
  • Red pulp = cords/sinusoids + macrophages. White pulp = PALS (T + DCs) and follicles (B). Marginal zone = interface; T-independent response to polysaccharides; immature <2 years → conjugate vaccines.
  • Asplenia: Howell–Jolly bodies, target cells, thrombocytosis; OPSI (pneumococcus most common; Hib, meningococcus, Capnocytophaga after dog bite; severe malaria, babesiosis). Vaccines + prophylaxis + alert card + emergency antibiotics.
  • MALT: largest lymphoid compartment; M cells; no capsule, no afferent lymphatics; tolerance by default; 3–5 g IgA/day; oral vaccines.
  • sIgA: dimer + J chain; pIgR transcytosis; secretory component protects from digestion; immune exclusion without inflammation; in breast milk. Selective IgA deficiency (1:300–700) = commonest primary immunodeficiency; false-negative coeliac serology; transfusion reactions.
  • Skin: keratinocytes (defensins), Langerhans cells, dermal DCs, TRM, mast cells; homing CLA + CCR4; contact dermatitis (type IV); psoriasis (IL-23/Th17).
  • Lymphatic disease: lymphoedema (filariasis commonest worldwide; Milroy = primary), sentinel node biopsy (melanoma, breast), lymphangitis (red streak), metastasis to regional nodes drives staging.
