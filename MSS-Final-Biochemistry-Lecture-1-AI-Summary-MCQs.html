1. Muscle types and functions

Three tissue types exist: skeletal (voluntary, striated, multinucleate, Ca²⁺ from sarcoplasmic reticulum [SR]; regulated by troponin and tropomyosin), cardiac (involuntary, striated, intercalated discs, autorhythmic, Ca²⁺ from SR and interstitial fluid), and smooth (involuntary, non-striated, Ca²⁺ regulated by calmodulin and myosin light-chain kinase, slowest contraction). Regeneration: skeletal limited (satellite cells); cardiac limited; smooth considerable (pericytes).

Functions: body movement, posture, moving substances (blood, digestive contents), heat generation (shivering), and fuel storage (largest store of glycogen and protein; glycogen is 1–2% of fresh muscle weight).

Skeletal fibres. Red fibres (type I): prolonged effort, aerobic metabolism, many mitochondria and myoglobin. White fibres (type II): fast, strong contractions, mainly anaerobic metabolism.

Handwritten: aerobic metabolism yields 30–32 ATP; anaerobic yields 2 ATP only (per glucose). An Arabic note beside red fibres is partly illegible but appears to say it "produces more energy".

2. Energy sources and the three ATP pathways

Muscle needs ATP (adenosine triphosphate) for the contraction cycle and for pumping Ca²⁺ into the SR. Stored ATP lasts only about 4–6 s (slide 30 states 1–2 s at exercise onset). At rest, fatty acids are the major fuel. Stored fuels: glycogen, phosphocreatine (PCr), ATP. Circulating fuels: glucose, fatty acids, amino acids, ketone bodies.

3. ATP–creatine phosphate system

ATP is poorly suited to storage because many enzymes are allosterically activated or inhibited by ATP level; PCr buffers ATP so that it stays constant. PCr + ADP ⇌ creatine + ATP, catalysed by creatine kinase (CK, also CPK). In contracting muscle the reaction regenerates ATP; in relaxed muscle it reverses. PCr supplies about 15 s of energy (slide 30: ~9 s of sustained exercise).

Synthesis. Three amino acids: glycine, arginine, methionine (as S-adenosylmethionine, SAM). Kidney: amidinotransferase forms guanidinoacetate (and ornithine). Liver: methyltransferase forms creatine. Creatine goes to brain, heart and skeletal muscle. PCr cyclises spontaneously and non-enzymatically to creatinine, which is excreted in urine; serum creatinine reflects muscle mass and renal clearance.

Handwritten: "Important" beside the ATP–PCr heading. ATP is not stored to avoid unnecessary activation or inhibition of reactions. Reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR) → less filtration → creatinine accumulates → ↑ serum creatinine. Creatinine is used as a marker of renal filtration.
Partly illegible: a note near PCr says (in Arabic) that no reaction takes energy directly from PCr; it seems to mean PCr works by regenerating ATP, not by being used directly. Verify.

4. Anaerobic respiration

Glucose (from glycogen or blood) → 2 pyruvate + 2 ATP + 2 NADH in the cytosol. With low O₂ the electron transport system (ETS) cannot reoxidise NADH, so lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) converts pyruvate to lactate and regenerates NAD⁺ for glycolysis. It is inefficient and accompanies conditions that contribute to fatigue. It dominates in burst sports such as basketball.

Handwritten: the main purpose of the pyruvate → lactate step is regeneration of NAD⁺. Lactate travels in blood to the liver (Cori cycle: lactate → pyruvate → glucose-6-phosphate [G6P] → glucose), and to kidney or heart, which oxidise it. A final note about glucose going to the brain is only partly legible.

Why glycogen gives 3 ATP. Glycogen enters as glucose-1-phosphate → G6P via phosphoglucomutase, bypassing the ATP-consuming hexokinase step (a handwritten box on the slide shows both routes).

Fatigue

Fatigue results from relative ATP deficit, sarcoplasmic pH fall to about 6.4, raised ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi), and ionic imbalance. H⁺ inhibits PFK-1 (phosphofructokinase-1) and myosin ATPase, and lowers troponin C Ca²⁺ sensitivity, reducing force. Glycogen is depleted in under 2 min of anaerobic exercise.

Oxygen debt

Post-exercise ventilation stays high to: restore myoglobin/haemoglobin O₂; oxidise lactate (to pyruvate → TCA, tricarboxylic acid cycle) or convert it to glucose in the liver; and fuel aerobic ATP for replenishing PCr and glycogen and running the Na⁺/K⁺ pump.

5. Aerobic respiration

Mitochondrial; fuels are pyruvate, fatty acids and amino acids; O₂ comes from blood haemoglobin and myoglobin. Products: ATP, CO₂, H₂O, heat. It provides over 90–95% of ATP in activity lasting more than 10 min.

6. Fatty acid metabolism and its regulation

Muscle does not synthesise fatty acids. Entry of fatty acyl-CoA into mitochondria via carnitine palmitoyltransferase I (CPT-I) is controlled by malonyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase-2 (ACC-2) makes malonyl-CoA from acetyl-CoA, which inhibits CPT-I. When energy falls, adenylate kinase (2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP) raises AMP, activating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK). AMPK phosphorylates and inactivates ACC-2 and activates malonyl-CoA decarboxylase (MCoADC), lowering malonyl-CoA, relieving CPT-I inhibition, and allowing fatty acid oxidation.

Phosphofructokinase-2 (PFK-2). In the liver, protein kinase A phosphorylation inhibits the kinase activity of the liver isoform. The skeletal muscle isoform lacks this regulatory site. The lecture lists adenosine monophosphate (AMP), fructose-6-phosphate, and inorganic phosphate (Pi) as activators and citrate as an inhibitor. Cardiac regulation differs, as described below.

7. Cardiac muscle fuels

Normal: fatty acids 60–80%, lactate and glucose 20–40%; about 98% of ATP is oxidative. Ketone bodies are not preferred (spared for the nervous system). Glucose enters via GLUT1 (~10%) and GLUT4 (~90%; stimulated by insulin and ischaemia). Cardiac PFK-2 is activated by an insulin kinase cascade and by AMPK.

Handwritten, why fatty acids? (1) energy dense; (2) continuous demand, the heart never rests; (3) spares glucose for tissues such as the brain that depend on it.

Ischaemia: anaerobic glycolysis rises (GLUT4 translocation), protons accumulate, and plasma free fatty acids increase. On reoxygenation, rapid fatty acid oxidation increases mitochondrial NADH and acetyl-CoA, which inhibit pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH); pyruvate and lactate build up, intracellular pH falls (metabolic acidosis), ion gradients need ATP to repair, and recovery is impaired.

8. Skeletal muscle fuels at rest and in starvation

Rest. Fatty acids supply about 60–70% of ATP (β-oxidation → TCA → ETC); glucose about 20–30%; amino acids under 5%. Glucose oxidation and fatty acid oxidation are reciprocally regulated by citrate: fatty acid oxidation raises citrate, which inhibits PFK-1 and glycolysis. Citrate leaving mitochondria activates ACC-2 → malonyl-CoA → inhibits CPT-I. At rest AMPK is inactive, so MCoADC is inactive.

Inconsistent in slides: fatty acids are given as 85% (slide 6), 60–80% (slide 25) and 60–70% (summary table). These estimates vary with nutritional state and fuel availability.

Starvation. Low insulin → less GLUT4 → muscle spares glucose for the nervous system. AMPK is active, ACC-2 inhibited, MCoADC activated, CPT-I fully active. High acetyl-CoA and NADH inhibit PDH. Ketone oxidation rises (succinyl-CoA:acetoacetate CoA transferase). Proteolysis rises early (amino acids for hepatic gluconeogenesis), then falls (protein sparing).

Handwritten: acetyl-CoA and NADH inhibit PDH, so less pyruvate is used and glycolysis falls. A note marked "CM" beside cardiac PFK-2 is unclear (possibly cardiac muscle).

9. Fuel use during exercise

ATP turnover can rise about 100-fold. Anaerobic glycolysis matters at the onset (ATP and PCr last only seconds; blood-borne fuels take over a minute to arrive), from glycogen, and in high intensity work.

Glycogenolysis control. PFK-1 and glycogen phosphorylase b are allosterically activated by AMP; AMP rises many-fold when ATP falls slightly because adenylate kinase keeps it low at rest. Muscle lacks glucagon receptors. Phosphorylase b is activated by: AMP binding (without phosphorylation); phosphorylation by phosphorylase kinase activated by Ca²⁺–calmodulin (full activation); or phosphorylation by phosphorylase kinase via epinephrine → cAMP → protein kinase A (PKA), which is slower.

Handwritten: PKA activates phosphorylase kinase (→ phosphorylase b to a) and inhibits glycogen synthase (inactivates glycogenesis). Epinephrine → ↑cAMP → PKA → glycogenolysis and glycolysis. Under low O₂, FA oxidation is strictly oxygen dependent because O₂ is the final electron acceptor in the ETC.

High intensity: PDH stays active while NADH is reoxidised; most pyruvate enters the TCA cycle and the remainder becomes lactate. Lactate fates: oxidation by resting muscle or heart (low NADH/NAD⁺ ratio favours pyruvate formation), or return to liver via the Cori cycle.

Mild to moderate, long exercise. Blood holds only about 5 g glucose, so the liver must replenish it. Hepatic glycogenolysis dominates for about 40 min, then output falls as fatty acid use rises (40–240 min). Lactate is the main gluconeogenic carbon source (also amino acids and glycerol). Hormonal profile: ↓insulin; ↑glucagon, epinephrine, norepinephrine; growth hormone, cortisol and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) also rise. Free fatty acids are the preferred fuel except just after eating, favoured by FFA availability (lipolysis), inhibition of glycolysis (acetyl-CoA, NADH, ATP inhibit PDH; high ATP lowers PFK-1 activity), possibly reduced GLUT4 transport, and ACC-2 inactivation by AMPK. Ketone bodies are minor.

Other fuels. Branched-chain amino acids (valine, leucine, isoleucine) give up to 20% of resting ATP and drive glutamine synthesis (nitrogen transport). The purine nucleotide cycle converts aspartate to fumarate plus ammonia; ammonia buffers protons. Acetate is activated to acetyl-CoA in the cytosol and enters mitochondria via acetylcarnitine transferase (CAT).

Handwritten: glutamine "transports nitrogen safely and manages acid–base homeostasis during metabolic stress"; purine nucleotide cycle: Asp → fumarate + acid–base balance. The graph slide (fuels over hours) shows glycogen peaking early then falling, while blood fatty acids rise to about 60% of O₂ uptake and glucose peaks near 40% then declines to exhaustion at about 4 h.
