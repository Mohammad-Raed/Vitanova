Lecture 1 · Disorders of skeletal muscle

Normal muscle and patterns of injury

Motor unit

  • One lower motor neuron + its axon
  • Neuromuscular junctions
  • The muscle fibres it supplies (all type I or all type II)

Fibre types

  • Type I slow myosin heavy chain, aerobic, high mitochondria, red, MYH7
  • Type II fast myosin, anaerobic, high glycogen, pale, MYH2/4/1
  • ATPase pH 9.4: type I light, type II dark
  • Anti-slow MHC stains type I brown; anti-fast MHC stains type II brown

Normal histology

  • Uniform polygonal myofibres
  • Peripheral nuclei
  • Fascicles with scant connective tissue
  • Normal = checkerboard of type I and II
Clue: central nuclei + variable fibre size = injury or degeneration.

Inherited vs acquired

Dystrophinopathies: Duchenne and Becker

Inherited

  • Muscular dystrophies: normal at birth, progressive injury
  • Congenital muscular dystrophies: progressive, early onset
  • Congenital myopathies: present in infancy, static or improving

Acquired (mainly adults)

  • Inflammatory myopathies
  • Toxic muscle injury
  • Postinfectious rhabdomyolysis
  • Muscle infarction in diabetes

Dystrophin

  • X-linked DMD gene, Xp21: males affected, females carriers
  • 427 kD; stabilises the sarcolemma in the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex
  • Present in skeletal and cardiac muscle, brain, peripheral nerves

Morphology

  • Ongoing necrosis and regeneration, marked size variation
  • Progressive replacement by fat and fibrosis
  • Calf pseudohypertrophy: early fibre hypertrophy, later fat and fibrosis
  • Cardiomyopathy in both

Clinical course

Pelvic girdle→Shoulder girdle→Distal, cardiac, respiratory
  • Waddling gait, stair difficulty, Gower’s sign
  • CK very high early, falls later as muscle is lost
  • Death: respiratory failure, pneumonia, cardiac decompensation
  • Cognitive impairment may occur

Polymyositis = CD8 endomysial; dermatomyositis = perifascicular atrophy and rash, screen for cancer in older adults; IBM = older male, rimmed vacuoles, steroid resistant.

Lecture 2 · Non-neoplastic diseases of bone

Bone structure and remodelling

Matrix

  • Osteoid (35%): type I collagen
  • Mineral (65%): hydroxyapatite, gives hardness
  • Cells: osteoblasts, osteocytes, osteoclasts

Woven vs lamellar

  • Woven: rapid, disorganised, cellular, weak. Normal in fetus and fracture repair; always abnormal in adults and children over 2–3 years
  • Lamellar: slow, parallel collagen fibres

Ossification

  • Long bone: endochondral, medullary cavity
  • Flat bone: intramembranous, no medullary cavity

RANKL / RANK / OPG

Osteoblast RANKL→RANK on precursor→NF-κB→Osteoclast formation and survival
  • OPG is a decoy receptor from osteoblasts that blocks RANKL. Raised by WNT (LRP5/6), BMPs, estrogen, testosterone.
  • RANKL is raised by PTH, vitamin D, IL-1, glucocorticoids.
  • The OPG : RANKL ratio sets bone resorption.
Metabolic bone disease

Osteoporosis, hyperparathyroidism, rickets

Definitions (DXA T-score)

  • Osteopenia −1 to −2.5
  • Osteoporosis −2.5 or lower
  • Peak bone mass in young adulthood; loss about 0.7% per year from the 4th decade

Primary types

  • Senile: low turnover; weak osteoblasts, low activity, low calcium and vitamin D
  • Postmenopausal: high turnover; estrogen loss → IL-1, IL-6, TNF → ↑RANKL, ↓OPG
  • Secondary: endocrine, GI, corticosteroids

Clinical and diagnosis

  • Vertebral fractures, height loss, kyphoscoliosis; femoral neck fracture → PE, pneumonia
  • Radiographs only show it after 30–40% loss
  • Calcium, phosphorus, ALP are normal
  • Use DXA or QCT
Treatment: exercise, calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonates (↓osteoclast activity), denosumab (anti-RANKL), hormone therapy.

Hyperparathyroidism

  • Primary: adenoma, hyperplasia, carcinoma
  • Secondary: chronic kidney disease (low active vitamin D, low calcium)
  • Tertiary also exists
  • ↑ Osteoclast activity → osteopenia → osteoporosis

Rickets and osteomalacia

  • Defective mineralisation, unmineralised osteoid accumulates
  • Vitamin D deficiency or abnormal metabolism → low calcium → secondary hyperparathyroidism
  • Rickets (children): bowed legs
  • Osteomalacia (adults): fractures
Increased but unsound bone

Paget disease of bone

Osteolytic→Mixed osteoclastic–osteoblastic→Osteosclerotic (quiescent)

Epidemiology and cause

  • Late adulthood; common in Western Europe and Australasia; rare in Asian, African, Middle Eastern descent
  • SQSTM1 mutations (50% familial, 10% sporadic) ↑NF-κB
  • Activating RANK or inactivating OPG mutations (juvenile Paget)
  • Possible viral trigger (measles / RNA virus)

Morphology

  • Mosaic pattern of lamellar bone with woven bone, prominent cement lines: pathognomonic
  • Thick bone with poor structural stability

Clinical

  • 85% polyostotic, 15% monostotic; axial skeleton and proximal femur
  • Often asymptomatic; ↑ALP with normal calcium
  • Chalk-stick fractures, secondary OA, leontiasis ossea, platybasia, spinal compression
  • Secondary osteosarcoma: under 1% overall, 5–10% in severe polyostotic disease
  • Treatment: calcitonin, bisphosphonates
Infection of bone and marrow

Osteomyelitis

SettingWhere / how
Child over 1 yearHematogenous to long-bone metaphysis (sluggish hairpin capillaries; physis is an avascular barrier)
Infant under 1 yearTransphyseal vessels allow spread to epiphysis and joint (septic arthritis)
AdultVertebrae and discs; also contiguous spread (diabetic foot) or direct implantation (open fracture, surgery)

Organisms

  • Staphylococcus aureus: 50–75%
  • E. coli, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella: urinary tract infection, IV drug use
  • Group B strep and E. coli: neonates
  • Salmonella: sickle cell disease
  • 20–40% have no organism isolated

Morphology

  • Neutrophils, bone and marrow necrosis, subperiosteal abscess, draining sinus
  • Sequestrum: dead bone
  • Involucrum: reactive new bone around it

Clinical

  • Fever, chills, leukocytosis, throbbing pain
  • X-ray: lytic focus with sclerotic rim
  • Biopsy and culture to identify the organism
  • Antibiotics + surgical drainage

Chronic osteomyelitis

Causes: delayed diagnosis, extensive necrosis, poor treatment, weak host defences. Complications: flare-ups, pathologic fracture, secondary amyloidosis, endocarditis, sepsis, squamous cell carcinoma in the sinus tract, sarcoma.

Mycobacterial and Pott disease

1–3% of TB patients have bone disease, usually blood-borne and solitary. Pott disease is tuberculous spondylitis: disc and vertebral destruction, kyphosis or scoliosis, cord compression. The spine is involved in 40%.

High-yield summary: woven bone in an adult is abnormal. Postmenopausal osteoporosis = ↑RANKL, normal labs. Paget = mosaic pattern, ↑ALP, chalk-stick fractures. Osteomyelitis = S. aureus, metaphysis in children, sequestrum inside involucrum.

Lecture 3 · Arthritis

Joints and osteoarthritis versus rheumatoid arthritis

Joint types

  • Solid (nonsynovial): fibrous and cartilaginous
  • Synovial: joint space, wide motion

Synovial lining

  • Type A macrophage-like
  • Type B fibroblast-like; makes hyaluronic acid
  • No basement membrane: easy exchange with blood

Main arthritides

OA (most common), RA, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, seronegative spondyloarthropathies, infectious arthritis, crystal arthritis (gout, pseudogout).

OA pathogenesis

Chondrocyte injury→↑ water, ↓ proteoglycan→MMPs degrade type II collagen→Chondrocyte loss, subchondral damage

Cytokines TGF-β, TNF, PGE₂ and NO contribute. Chronic low-grade synovitis drives progression. Secondary OA is only about 5% and occurs in younger people.

RA pathogenesis

  • Genes (about 50%): HLA-DRB1, PTPN22
  • Environment (smoking, infection) → citrullination of self proteins
  • CD4+ Th17 / Th1 cells (IL-17, IFN-γ, RANKL)
  • Macrophages: TNF and IL-1; TNF is the key target

RA serology and imaging

  • ACPA positive in 70%, highly specific
  • RF positive in 80%, also in healthy elderly and other diseases
  • X-ray: effusion, juxta-articular osteopenia, erosions, narrowed space

Rheumatoid nodules

Subcutaneous (forearm, elbows, occiput, lumbosacral). Histology: necrotizing granuloma. Rarely lung nodules or interstitial lung disease.

Risks of therapy

Long-term TNF antagonists raise infection risk, especially TB. Deaths relate to vasculitis, amyloidosis, GI bleeding (NSAIDs) and infection (immunosuppression).

Seronegative spondyloarthropathies

Enthesitis, sacroiliac joints and HLA-B27

RF negative·Enthesitis·Sacroiliac involvement·HLA-B27 (MHC I)·Ankylosis·CD8+ T cells

Ankylosing spondylitis

  • Prototype; young males (2nd–3rd decade)
  • Low back pain, spinal immobility, sacroiliac ankylosis
  • About 90% HLA-B27; peripheral joints in one-third
  • Treatment: NSAIDs, anti-IL-17, TNF inhibitors

Reactive arthritis

  • Triad: arthritis, nongonococcal urethritis/cervicitis, conjunctivitis
  • Men in 20s–30s; 60–80% HLA-B27
  • After Chlamydia or GI infection (Shigella, Salmonella, Yersinia, Campylobacter)
  • Asymmetric oligoarthritis of ankles, knees, feet
Crystal-induced arthritis

Gout and pseudogout

Uric acid metabolism

  • End product of purine breakdown; xanthine oxidase makes it (allopurinol target)
  • PRPP synthetase overactivity: overproduction
  • HGPRT deficiency (salvage pathway): overproduction; complete loss = Lesch-Nyhan, X-linked
  • Excretion: 70% urine, 30% gut; proximal tubule reabsorbs

Causes of hyperuricemia

  • Primary: reduced renal excretion (URAT1, GLUT9) is most common; overproduction is a minority
  • Secondary: cell turnover (chemotherapy, tumour lysis, haemolysis), kidney disease, thiazide / loop diuretics

Why only some get gout

Urate above 6.8 mg/dL is necessary but not sufficient; only about 10% develop gout, usually after 20–30 years. Risks: male, menopause, alcohol, obesity, genes, renal failure.

Mechanism

Urate crystals→Macrophage NLRP3 inflammasome→IL-1β→Neutrophil influx and lysis→Acute arthritis

Four stages

1Asymptomatic hyperuricemia (puberty in men, after menopause in women)
2Acute arthritis: sudden severe pain, usually monoarticular, 50% first MTP joint (podagra)
3Intercritical period: symptom-free; attacks recur and become polyarticular if untreated
4Chronic tophaceous gout: about 12 years later; tophi, joint destruction, pannus

Gouty nephropathy: urate in the renal medulla; stones and pyelonephritis. Tophus: urate aggregate with fibroblasts, mononuclear and giant cells.
