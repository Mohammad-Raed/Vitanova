Introduction to immunity

The whole immune system in one lecture: definitions, four lines of defence, innate vs adaptive, humoral vs cell-mediated, memory and tolerance.

1. Explanation2. High-yield summary3. Practice MCQs
Part 1

Explanation

Objectives

  • Define immunity, antigen, epitope, antibody, serology and vaccination.
  • Place the 1st and 2nd lines (innate) and the 3rd and 4th lines (adaptive) of defence.
  • Contrast innate and adaptive immunity; humoral and cell-mediated immunity; active and passive immunity.
  • Explain specificity, diversity, memory and self-tolerance.
  • Trace key milestones from Jenner to 2026 and map the course ahead.

Why the immune system matters

Defends against microbes

Viruses, bacteria, fungi and parasites.

Watches for tumour cells

Recognises and removes them.

Can cause problems

Transplant rejection, and antibodies against biologic drugs.

Recognises almost anything

Antibodies can bind proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, nucleic acids and small chemicals (haptens).

Clean-up and repair

Clears dead cells and supports tissue repair.

Can turn on self

Autoimmune disease affects about 1 in 10 people (Conrad et al., Lancet 2023).

Clarifying a confusing pair: every immunogen is an antigen, but not every antigen is an immunogen. An antigen is anything an antibody or TCR can bind; an immunogen is one that actually triggers a response. A hapten is the classic example of an antigen that is not an immunogen on its own.

Four lines of defence

LineWhat it isPart of
1Physical and chemical barriers: skin, mucous membranes and their secretions.Innate
2Phagocytic cells. Encapsulated bacteria can evade phagocytosis and get past this line.Innate
3Humoral immunity: antibodies target extracellular bacteria and help overcome the capsule by enhancing phagocytosis.Adaptive

Flag: the slide shows a graphic labelling lymphocytes. The note-taker adds that NK cells and innate lymphoid cells are lymphocytes that still belong to innate immunity, which matters because “lymphocyte” does not automatically mean “adaptive”.

Innate immunity

  • Present in healthy individuals and ready before infection; acts within minutes to hours.
  • Recognises PAMPs (pathogen-associated patterns on microbes) and DAMPs (from damaged cells) through pattern-recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptors.
  • Components: epithelial barriers and antimicrobial peptides; phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages); dendritic cells, NK cells, innate lymphoid cells; complement; inflammatory cytokines (TNF, IL-1, IL-6).
  • It is generally described as having no memory, although “trained immunity” shows that innate cells can be reprogrammed.

The splinter story (slide 7)

Splinter breaks the skin→Same response to any bacterium that enters→Starts within minutes; inflammation within hours→A second splinter triggers the same response

The point: the innate response does not change with experience. The second splinter gets the same reaction as the first.

Adaptive immunity

  • Responds to microbes or antigens that enter tissues; carried out by B cells and T cells and their products.
  • Distinguishes closely related molecules (very specific).
  • Takes days (about 5–7) to reach its peak; protection results from prior experience with the antigen.
  • Jenner: material from cowpox lesions protected a boy against smallpox. This was the first vaccine (vacca = cow) and showed antigen specificity, a slower first response and long-lived memory.
Flag: the slide says “about 5–7 days to peak” here, while the memory slide gives a “lag of 5–10 days” for the primary response. These measure different things (time to peak vs delay before the response begins) and neither is explained in detail, so use the number that matches the slide you are looking at.

The slide’s timeline graphic of immunology milestones is not available as text, so dates are not repeated here.

Dendritic cells: the bridge, and the three signals

  • Antigen-presenting cells (APCs) process antigens and present them on MHC molecules to activate T lymphocytes. Main types: dendritic cells, macrophages, B lymphocytes.
  • Dendritic cells sit in tissues and migrate through lymphatics to lymph nodes.
  • MHC class I → CD8⁺ T cells; MHC class II → CD4⁺ T cells.

Naïve T cells need three signals

1 · TCR binds peptide–MHC (specificity)→2 · Co-stimulation (B7–CD28)→3 · Cytokines direct the type of response

Clinical links on the slide: HLA matching in transplantation, and checkpoint inhibitors that act on these signals.

Humoral immunity (B cells and antibodies)

  • Antibodies are produced by B lymphocytes → plasma cells and travel in blood and mucosal fluids.
  • They act mainly against extracellular microbes and microbial toxins.
  • Functions: neutralisation of viruses and toxins; opsonisation for phagocytosis; complement activation; ADCC by NK cells.

Why antibodies are not enough alone: the lecturer pointed out (per the note-taker) that antibodies cannot kill an antigen that sits inside cells. That is the job of T cells, which is the next part.

Cell-mediated immunity (T cells)

  • Does not act through antibodies. Targets intracellular microbes (viruses, Mycobacterium tuberculosis) and tumour cells.
  • CD4⁺ helper T cells secrete cytokines: Th1 activate macrophages; Th2 eosinophils; Th17 neutrophils; Tfh help B cells make high-affinity antibodies.
  • CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells kill infected or tumour cells using perforin and granzymes.
  • Regulatory T cells (Tregs) restrain responses and protect self.

Memory trick from the slide: Active = your body makes antibodies. Passive = you receive ready-made antibodies. Natural = without medical administration. Artificial = through medical intervention.

Clonal selection and diversity

  • About 10⁷–10⁹ distinct lymphocyte clones together recognise a vast range of antigens.
  • Diversity is generated by V(D)J gene recombination.
  • Each clone carries receptors for one epitope; antigen binds and activates only the matching clone.
  • Activation gives effector cells (clear the antigen) and memory cells (persist).
  • Clinical links: RAG gene defects → SCID; CAR-T cells receive engineered specificity.
  • Naïve cells: mature lymphocytes that have not yet met their antigen. Memory cells: long-lived cells formed after earlier exposure that respond faster and more strongly.

Primary vs secondary response

Clinical uses: booster doses, and IgM vs IgG serology to tell a recent from a past infection.

Self-tolerance

  • Strongly self-reactive lymphocytes are deleted in the thymus (T cells) and bone marrow (B cells).
  • In the periphery, tolerance is maintained by anergy, deletion and suppression by regulatory T cells (FOXP3 Tregs).
  • Failure → autoimmune disease (e.g. type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, MS).
  • AIRE defect → APS-1; FOXP3 defect → IPEX.
  • The slide marks a Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine to Brunkow, Ramsdell and Sakaguchi for discoveries on peripheral immune tolerance (regulatory T cells).

When immunity goes wrong: four categories

Hypersensitivity

Too much response to harmless antigens.

Autoimmunity

Loss of self-tolerance.

Immunodeficiency

Absent or weak responses.

Immunoproliferative disease

Malignant immune cells.

Milestones on the slides

  • Personalised cancer vaccines in phase 3.
  • Checkpoint inhibitors release T-cell brakes on tumours.
  • CAR-T cells: leukaemia, myeloma, and lupus trials.
  • RSV protection for infants (nirsevimab).

Part 2

High-yield summary

  • Immunity = resistance to disease; antigen = anything an antibody/TCR binds; immunogen = antigen that induces a response; epitope = the part bound by one antibody; serology = antigen–antibody testing of serum.
  • Lines 1–2 = innate (barriers, phagocytes); lines 3–4 = adaptive (humoral, cell-mediated).
  • Innate: minutes–hours, germline PRRs (TLRs), recognises PAMPs/DAMPs, no memory (trained immunity aside). Adaptive: days, rearranged BCR/TCR (~10⁷–10⁹ clones), memory.
  • Dendritic cells bridge the two. MHC I → CD8, MHC II → CD4. Three signals: TCR–peptide–MHC, co-stimulation (B7–CD28), cytokines.
  • Humoral = B cells/antibodies vs extracellular microbes and toxins (neutralise, opsonise, activate complement, ADCC). IgM first; IgG main and crosses the placenta; IgA mucosa/milk; IgE allergy/helminths; IgD naïve BCR.
  • Cell-mediated = T cells vs intracellular microbes and tumours. Th1 → macrophages; Th2 → eosinophils; Th17 → neutrophils; Tfh → B cells; CD8 → perforin/granzyme killing; Treg → restraint.
  • Active = own response, slow, long, memory (vaccines). Passive = ready-made antibody, immediate, short, no memory (maternal IgG, IgA milk, HBIG, rabies Ig, antivenom, nirsevimab).
  • Primary: lag 5–10 d, IgM. Secondary: lag 1–3 d, IgG, 10–100× greater and longer-lasting.
  • Tolerance: central (thymus/bone marrow) + peripheral (anergy, deletion, Tregs). AIRE → APS-1; FOXP3 → IPEX; RAG → SCID.
  • Four failures: hypersensitivity, autoimmunity, immunodeficiency, immunoproliferative disease.
