Big picture

From stimulus to reflex

Stimulus→Sensory transduction→Afferent transmission→Synapse(s)→Motor output (efferent)

The same afferent input also travels up to the brain, so sensation and perception run in parallel with the reflex.

Definition: a somatic reflex is an automatic response produced by skeletal muscle contraction without conscious effort, for example withdrawing from a painful stimulus.
Sensory transduction

Receptor potentials

What happens

  • A stimulus changes receptor permeability, usually opening channels so that Na⁺ enters and the receptor depolarizes.
  • The result is a graded receptor potential.
  • It has no refractory period, so rapid successive stimuli can summate.

Stimulus strength coding

Stronger stimulus→Bigger permeability change→Larger receptor potential→Higher AP frequency→More neurotransmitter released

Strength is coded by firing rate, not by AP size.

Muscle spindle structure

  • Intrafusal fibres wrapped by sensory endings.
  • Ia fibres respond to change in length.
  • Group II fibres carry static length.
  • Large-diameter, myelinated afferents.
  • Intrafusal fibres are innervated by gamma motoneurons.
  • Extrafusal fibres (the bulk of the muscle) are innervated by alpha motoneurons.
  • Gamma input sets spindle tension and therefore sensitivity.
Group Ia reflex

The stretch reflex

Also called the monosynaptic reflex, tendon tap reflex or knee jerk (patellar tendon reflex).

Tap patellar tendon→Quadriceps spindle stretched→Ia afferent to cord→1 synapse on alpha motoneuron→Quadriceps contracts

Circuit

  • Spindles detect stretch → CNS (afferent).
  • Motoneurons of the stretched muscle and synergists are activated (efferent).
  • Antagonists are inhibited via an inhibitory interneuron (disynaptic). In the knee jerk the hamstring is inhibited.

Facts to know

  • Almost all muscles show a stretch reflex; the patellar one is just convenient.
  • Graded: a harder tap gives a larger response, up to a point.
  • Ia axon collaterals also travel up to the brain.
  • Electrical stimulation of the nerve produces the H reflex (Hoffmann).
  • Normal movement of the limb also elicits it, not only a tap.
Function: rapid correction of unexpected muscle stretch (can stop you falling) and sensory feedback that helps activate muscles during movement. It is a negative feedback loop: contraction relieves the stretch and spindle firing falls.
Gain control

How can the stretch reflex be controlled?

SiteMechanism
1. Sensory afferent (spindle)Gamma motoneurons adjust intrafusal tension. More gamma activity means higher spindle tone, sensitivity and output.
2. The synapsePresynaptic inhibition: GABA-ergic axo-axonal synapses reduce transmitter release from the afferent terminal.
3. The motoneuronGreater excitability gives a larger response (more recruitment, more APs). Example: the Jendrassik manoeuvre adds background excitation and enhances the reflex.

Alpha-gamma coactivation

  • If only alpha motoneurons fire, the extrafusal muscle shortens and the spindle goes slack, so Ia fibres fall silent.
  • Firing alpha and gamma together keeps the spindle taut and responsive across a range of muscle lengths.

Presynaptic inhibition in one example

Terminal A excites cell C. Inhibitory terminal B sits on terminal A. Stimulating A + B gives no EPSP in C, but stimulating D + B still produces an EPSP, because B acts only on A. It silences one input without touching the others.

Postsynaptic summation (revision)

Temporal

The same input fires again before the first EPSP has died away, so the EPSPs add.

Spatial

Two or more different excitatory inputs fire together and their EPSPs add. An EPSP plus an IPSP cancel.

Group Ib reflex

Golgi tendon organs

  • When the GTO is stretched (usually by muscle contraction), collagen fibres compress the Ib axon and its firing rate rises.
  • At rest: Ib input inhibits motoneurons of the same muscle through Ib inhibitory interneurons, roughly the opposite of the stretch reflex.
  • The old idea was that this protects muscle from excessive force. That view is outdated.
State-dependent reflex reversal: during locomotion the Ib inhibitory interneurons are inhibited and Ib excitatory interneurons are facilitated (McCrea and colleagues). The Ib action on extensor motoneurons flips from inhibition to excitation, reinforcing motor activity and force production.

Why is it complicated?

Ib interneurons also receive input from muscle spindles, joint receptors, cutaneous receptors and descending pathways. So the effect is inhibitory at rest and excitatory during action.

Proprioceptive reflexes in movement: stretch reflexes correct unexpected length changes. Spindle and GTO afferents together give ongoing feedback that reinforces or modifies the motor pattern, for example when you stumble.
Polysynaptic reflexes

Withdrawal and crossed extension

Flexion withdrawal reflex

Painful stimulus→Nociceptor→Afferent via dorsal root→Interneuron(s) in cord→Flexor motoneurons fire→Limb withdrawn
  • At least disynaptic, possibly polysynaptic.
  • Activates flexors of the whole limb and inhibits extensors (elbow example: biceps contracts, triceps relaxes).
  • The cord also sends the signal up an ascending pathway for pain perception.
  • The reflex happens before you perceive the pain.

Crossed extension reflex

  • Starts at the same time as a leg withdrawal reflex.
  • Contralateral effect: nociceptive afferent → interneuron(s) → opposite-side extensor motoneurons.
  • Activates contralateral extensors, inhibits contralateral flexors.
  • Result: the opposite leg extends and supports body weight so the injured leg can lift. It stops you falling when you step on something sharp.

High-yield summary

Last-minute checklist

Transduction

  • Receptor potential is graded with no refractory period.
  • Stronger stimulus → higher AP frequency.
  • Afferent AP starts at the peripheral end; interneuron and efferent AP at the axon hillock.

Cord

  • Dorsal = afferent. Ventral = efferent.
  • Gray matter has cell bodies; white matter has axons.

Spindle

  • Parallel, senses length, Ia (change) and II (static).
  • Intrafusal ← gamma; extrafusal ← alpha.
  • Alpha-gamma coactivation prevents slack.

GTO

  • Series, senses tension, Ib afferent.
  • Inhibitory at rest, excitatory in locomotion.
  • The “protective” role is outdated.

Stretch reflex

  • Monosynaptic to the same muscle; antagonist inhibited via an interneuron.
  • Controlled at spindle (gamma), synapse (presynaptic inhibition) and motoneuron (Jendrassik).

Polysynaptic

  • Withdrawal: ipsilateral flexors on, extensors off, before pain is felt.
  • Crossed extension: contralateral extensors on, flexors off.
  • Interneurons add a control site.
