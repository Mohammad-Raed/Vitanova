Epitopes: valence, dominance, mimicry

Slide 5

The immune response is directed at a small part of the antigen, the epitope, not the whole organism. One antigen usually carries several epitopes. A microbe can have many antigens (cell wall, pili, flagella, DNA, RNA of viruses).

  • B cells and T cells see different things. Antibodies and BCRs bind conformational surface shapes; T cells see short linear peptides held by MHC.
  • Valence: number of epitopes on one antigen. High valence cross-links B-cell receptors strongly, which is one way B cells are activated.
  • Immunodominance: a few epitopes attract most of the response. If a virus mutates an immunodominant epitope, it escapes immunity (immune escape).
  • Cryptic epitopes: hidden inside the molecule until tissue damage or processing exposes them.
  • Epitope spreading: the response widens from one epitope to others as damage continues; typical of autoimmune disease.
  • Cross-reactivity and molecular mimicry: a microbial epitope resembling a self epitope can trigger disease. Rheumatic fever after streptococcal infection; Guillain-Barré syndrome after Campylobacter.
  • Mapping epitopes: peptide arrays and structural methods guide vaccine and antibody design.
Annotation · EpitopesEpitope: small part of the antigen that the response targets; one antigen may carry about 20. Response may focus on one or two (immunodominant); mutation there → immune escape. Epitope shared by several microorganisms → cross-reactivity → false-positive serological test. Mimicry examples (rheumatic fever, Guillain-Barré): marked important.

Haptens and drug-induced responses

Slide 6
  • A hapten is too small to be immunogenic alone but becomes immunogenic when bound to a carrier protein (hapten + carrier → response).
  • Once antibody exists, the free hapten can still be bound; this is how drug allergy tests work.
  • Poison ivy: urushiol binds skin proteins → T-cell-mediated contact dermatitis 24–72 h later (type IV hypersensitivity).

Adjuvants: turning an antigen into a vaccine

Slides 7–8

An adjuvant is a substance added to a vaccine that strengthens the immune response by improving the antigen’s immunogenicity.

  • Why needed: purified proteins are poorly immunogenic without an innate signal. A whole microorganism carries its own danger signals; a purified protein does not.
  • Role: the adjuvant acts like an alarm bell, alerting innate immunity, which in turn gives the appropriate activation signals for adaptive immunity. The antigen still needs the right epitope; an adjuvant cannot rescue a poor antigen.
  • How they work: (1) depot effect: antigen stays at the injection site for longer, so immune cells have prolonged access; (2) recruit and activate dendritic cells (the main APC) through pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs, e.g. TLR4, TLR9), which take the antigen to T lymphocytes.
  • Trade-off: reactogenicity. A sore arm and fever reflect innate activation.

MHC (HLA): genetics and structure

Slides 3, 8–10, 15, 19

The major histocompatibility complex (MHC), encoded by HLA (human leukocyte antigen) genes, presents antigen fragments to T cells and binds T-cell receptors (TCRs). It is co-dominant (both parental alleles expressed on every cell) and polygenic (several genes).

  • Location: ~4-million-base cluster on chromosome 6p21, the most gene-dense region of the genome.
  • Class I loci: HLA-A, -B, -C (one letter). Class II loci: HLA-DP, -DQ, -DR (two letters). The subtypes are important in transplantation.
  • Class III: complement C4, C2, factor B and TNF; mainly complement; not presentation molecules.
  • TAP and immunoproteasome (PSMB) genes also lie in the MHC and serve the class I pathway.
  • Haplotype inheritance: a whole set is inherited as a block from each parent → 25% chance that two siblings match (0.5 × 0.5). Linkage disequilibrium: certain haplotypes are commoner than chance.

Antigen processing and presentation

Slides 11–12, 16–17

Rule: endogenous → MHC I → CD8⁺ (viruses); exogenous → MHC II → CD4⁺ T helper (extracellular pathogens). The best-known adaptive cell that attacks viruses is the cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cell.

Class I (endogenous) pathway

  1. Class I molecules are synthesised in the rough ER.
  2. Cytosolic proteins (viral, tumour or self) are degraded by the proteasome (IFN-γ switches in the immunoproteasome).
  3. TAP1/TAP2 (transporter associated with antigen processing) pump peptides into the ER.
  4. ERAP (ER aminopeptidase) trims peptides to the right length (marked important).
  5. Tapasin, calreticulin and ERp57 load the peptide onto class I + β2m.
  6. The complex travels via the Golgi to the surface and is shown to CD8⁺ T cells.

Failures: TAP deficiency → bare lymphocyte syndrome type I (low MHC I). CMV, HSV and HIV block TAP or remove MHC I. Tumours lose HLA or β2m; NK cells then kill the MHC-low cell.

Class II (exogenous) pathway

  1. Class II is expressed mainly by dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells.
  2. Extracellular antigen is taken up by phagocytosis or endocytosis.
  3. Phagosomes fuse with lysosomes; acid proteases (cathepsins) degrade the antigen.
  4. Class II is made in the RER and moves via the Golgi toward endosomes.
  5. The invariant chain blocks the groove and prevents premature peptide binding (it acts like a lock). It is degraded, leaving CLIP, which HLA-DM (regulated by HLA-DO) exchanges for peptide, in the MIIC compartment.
  6. The complex goes to the surface for CD4⁺ T cells.

Acidification is essential: without lysosomal acidification, processing and loading would fail. Autophagy also delivers cytosolic protein to class II. Cross-presentation: cDC1 dendritic cells place external antigen on class I to prime CD8⁺ cells; essential for tumour and viral vaccines. Bare lymphocyte syndrome type II (CIITA/RFX defect) → no class II, no CD4 help.

MHC restriction

Slide 13
  • Experiment (Zinkernagel and Doherty, 1974; Nobel Prize 1996): killer T cells from an infected mouse killed infected targets only if the MHC matched. Different MHC + infected → not killed; same MHC, uninfected → not killed.
  • Conclusion: T cells need both the right peptide and the right self-MHC.
  • Consequences: (1) thymic selection must teach T cells to recognise self-MHC; (2) alloreactivity: 1–10% of T cells respond to a foreign MHC, so unmatched grafts are rejected quickly; (3) engineered T-cell therapies work only in patients with the matching HLA type.

Beyond peptides: CD1 and MR1

Slide 15

Not every antigen is a protein or peptide. CD1 and MR1 are antigen-presenting molecules distinct from MHC I and II.

MoleculePresentsTo
CD1 (class I-like; deep hydrophobic groove)Lipids, glycolipids—
CD1a–cMycobacterial lipids (mycolic acid, lipoarabinomannan); relevant to TBT cells
CD1dGlycolipidsiNKT cells → rapid IFN-γ and IL-4
MR1 (MHC class I-related protein 1)Bacterial and fungal vitamin B2 metabolitesMAIT cells (1–10% of blood T cells; rich in liver and gut)

CD1 and MR1 are non-polymorphic: everyone presents the same lipids and metabolites, unlike HLA-A1, A2, A3, which differ between people. This suggests “HLA-independent” vaccines. These cells respond within hours (innate-like speed). iNKT and MAIT cells use semi-invariant TCRs.

Applications, HLA in practice and case

Slides 16–18

Designing antigens (2026)

  • Structure-based design: fusion proteins locked in the prefusion shape (RSV F, SARS-CoV-2 spike) expose the best neutralising epitopes.
  • Epitope prediction: sequence + HLA type → machine-learning prediction of binding. Immunopeptidomics: mass spectrometry of peptides eluted from MHC confirms what is presented.
  • Personalised mRNA neoantigen vaccines (with anti-PD-1; phase 3); germline-targeting immunogens for HIV broadly neutralising antibodies; tebentafusp targets gp100 on HLA-A*02:01.

HLA beyond transplantation

  • NK surveillance: HLA-E and classical class I signal “healthy cell”; losing them triggers missing-self killing.
  • Pregnancy: HLA-G on trophoblast inhibits maternal NK cells.
  • Cancer escape: loss of one HLA haplotype or of β2m prevents presentation and contributes to checkpoint-inhibitor failure.
  • Transfusion: donor anti-HLA antibodies cause TRALI (transfusion-related acute lung injury); patient anti-HLA antibodies cause platelet refractoriness, needing HLA-matched platelets.
  • HIV: B*57:01 and B*27:05 are enriched among controllers without treatment.

Case: infant who cannot present to CD4⁺ T cells

7-month-old, consanguineous family: chronic diarrhoea (Cryptosporidium), failure to thrive, two pneumonias, oral candidiasis, no vaccine response. CD4⁺ 180/µL (low), CD8⁺ and B cells normal, IgG low, HLA-DR absent on B cells and monocytes.

  • Diagnosis: combined immunodeficiency with low CD4 → MHC class II deficiency (bare lymphocyte syndrome II).
  • Mechanism: defect in a class II transcription factor (CIITA, RFX5, RFXANK, RFXAP) → no class II on any APC.
  • Consequence: poor CD4⁺ selection in the thymus and no T-cell help → no antibody.
  • Treatment: prophylaxis and immunoglobulin, then haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation; screen siblings.

High-yield revision summary

  • Auto = self; allo = same species; xeno = other species; heterophile = cross-species (Monospot/EBV); superantigen bypasses the groove.
  • Antibodies see conformational shapes; T cells see linear peptides + MHC.
  • Epitope spreading = autoimmunity; mimicry = rheumatic fever (Strep), Guillain-Barré (Campylobacter).
  • Hapten needs a carrier; urushiol = type IV; drug routes: hapten, pro-hapten, p-i, altered repertoire.
  • Adjuvants: depot + innate signal; alum = antibody; AS01 = T cells (shingles, malaria); MF59 = squalene; CpG 1018 = TLR9 (hepatitis B).
  • MHC on chromosome 6p21; class III = complement/TNF; siblings match 25%.
  • Class I: α + β2m (chr 15), closed groove 8–10 aa, all nucleated cells, CD8. Class II: α + β, open groove 13–25 aa, APCs, CD4.
  • Class I pathway: proteasome → TAP → ERAP → ER loading. Class II: invariant chain → CLIP → HLA-DM, needs acidification.
  • TAP defect = BLS I; CIITA/RFX defect = BLS II (low CD4, no antibody).
  • Dendritic cells prime naive T cells; cDC1 cross-presents to CD8; cDC2 primes CD4.
  • CD1 = lipids; CD1d → iNKT; MR1 = vitamin B2 metabolites → MAIT; both non-polymorphic.
  • Missing self (HLA-E, class I loss) → NK killing; HLA-G protects the fetus; patient anti-HLA → platelet refractoriness; donor anti-HLA → TRALI.
