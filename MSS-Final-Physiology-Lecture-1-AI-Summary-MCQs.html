1. From stimulus to reflex

A somatic reflex is an automatic skeletal muscle response to a stimulus. A receptor converts the stimulus into an electrical signal (sensory transduction). An afferent fibre carries the signal toward the spinal cord. One or more spinal synapses route it to an efferent motor neuron, which activates skeletal muscle.

Stimulus & receptor→Afferent fibre→Spinal synapse(s)→Motor neuron→Muscle

At the same time, ascending signals can reach the brain and contribute to conscious sensation. You can begin withdrawing before you consciously register the pain.

ReflexSensor and pathwayResult
StretchMuscle spindle senses length and change in length. Group Ia is the primary spindle afferent class. It directly excites alpha motor neurons of the stretched muscle.The stretched muscle contracts. In parallel, an inhibitory interneuron relaxes the antagonist (reciprocal inhibition). Only the direct excitatory limb is monosynaptic.

Walk it through: Step on a sharp object with your right foot. The right leg flexes away, and the left leg extends to support you. Remember: same side escapes; opposite side supports. A patellar tendon tap, by contrast, stretches the quadriceps spindle even though the hammer hits the tendon.

2. Tone and motor neuron lesions

Lower motor neurons (LMNs)

Cell bodies lie in the spinal cord’s ventral horn or motor nuclei of the brainstem. Their axons directly innervate skeletal muscle. An alpha motor neuron and the muscle fibres it supplies form a motor unit. A lesion interrupts the final route to contraction.

Upper motor neurons (UMNs)

Neurons in the motor cortex, including primary motor and premotor regions, and descending motor centres in the brainstem influence spinal and cranial motor circuits. Corticospinal fibres act through interneurons and, in some pathways, directly on motor neurons. They help set reflex strength and pattern.

Why a relaxed limb still resists movement

Muscle tone is the resistance felt during passive movement. Muscle and connective tissue supply passive elastic resistance; ongoing neural activity and stretch reflexes add an active component, especially during posture. Descending commands and postural reflexes adjust this activity to stabilize the body.

Fasciculation: spontaneous discharge of a motor unit, often arising in its motor axon or terminal, may make a visible twitch. Fibrillation: spontaneous activity in a single muscle fibre, often after denervation; usually detected with needle EMG rather than seen through the skin.

Examples: Polio can injure anterior horn cells. Guillain–Barré syndrome affects peripheral nerves and often produces areflexic weakness. Botulism reduces acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction and can mimic a flaccid LMN pattern without destroying LMNs. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) can show both UMN and LMN findings.

3. Descending control changes strength and pattern

Strength: how much reflex output follows a given input?

Reflex gain describes the size of the response for a given input. Descending pathways can inhibit or facilitate spinal circuits. When inhibitory regulation is lost, an established UMN lesion often produces hyperreflexia. Immediately after severe spinal cord injury, spinal shock can temporarily suppress reflexes before they return and become brisk.

During the Jendrassik manoeuvre, the patient hooks the fingers and pulls while the examiner taps the tendon. This can reinforce a difficult-to-elicit tendon reflex. It illustrates that descending or task-related effects can also facilitate reflexes.

Pattern: which movement does the stimulus produce?

Stroking the lateral sole and curving toward the forefoot normally produces toe flexion or no clear response in adults. In an extensor plantar response, the great toe extends upward; the other toes may fan. The sign suggests impaired corticospinal control in an adult. In infants it can be normal while descending pathways mature.

Distinguish a reproducible great-toe extension from a rapid whole-leg withdrawal to an unpleasant stimulus. Brisk tendon reflexes describe gain; Babinski describes a change in pattern.

Spasticity: resistance rises when a relaxed limb is stretched faster because stretch reflexes are overactive. Stroke or spinal cord injury can cause it. Both mono- and polysynaptic pathways may contribute to abnormal reflex behaviour. A fixed contracture can also increase passive resistance, but is mechanically different from reflex spasticity.

4. Why baclofen can reduce spasticity

Baclofen activates the type B receptor for gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), written GABAB. The spinal result is weaker transmission through reflex circuits, including mono- and polysynaptic responses. The diagram’s two forms of inhibition act at different sites:

SiteMechanismEffect
PresynapticAn axon can contact a sensory terminal (an axo-axonic synapse). GABAB signalling can reduce calcium entry into the terminal.Less excitatory transmitter released; the downstream excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) is smaller. Other terminals onto the same cell can still transmit.
PostsynapticGABAB signalling can increase potassium conductance on a downstream neuron.It becomes harder to fire. Interneurons and motor neurons are less readily recruited.
Diagram checkpoint: what happens if a different excitatory input fires?

In the slide example, an inhibitory terminal acts on excitatory terminal A before it reaches cell C. The response to A decreases; an independent terminal D can still excite C. If another excitatory input increases transmitter release specifically at D, D can produce a larger EPSP, other things being equal. This illustrates input-specific modulation.

Receptor distinction: The slide’s chloride diagram illustrates fast inhibition via GABAA or glycine receptors. Chloride conductance can hyperpolarize or shunt excitatory input, depending on the chloride gradient. Baclofen acts at GABAB, a receptor that signals indirectly via G proteins.

Why intrathecal? Baclofen crosses the blood–brain barrier poorly. Oral baclofen can still work, but intrathecal delivery places the drug in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) around the spinal cord, achieving effective spinal concentrations with lower systemic exposure. This is the physiological rationale for selected cases of severe spasticity.

5. How walking gets its rhythm

Central pattern generators

A central pattern generator (CPG) is a network in the central nervous system (CNS) that can generate an organized rhythmic motor pattern when activated, even without rhythmic sensory input. In the spinal locomotor network, interneurons deliver alternating bursts of excitation and inhibition to flexor and extensor motor neurons. Descending drive can initiate or change the pattern; the cortex does not have to issue a separate command for every step.

Fictive locomotion is a locomotion-like pattern recorded from motor nerves without actual stepping. Alternating flexor and extensor bursts show that the core rhythm can be generated centrally. Real walking still depends on sensory feedback for timing, strength and stability.

Feedback roleMechanism and example
RetimesStance is when the limb supports weight; swing is when it advances. Hip extension stretches hip flexors near the end of stance. Proprioceptive input from muscles and joints can advance flexor bursts and help trigger swing. In the cat traces, passive hip motion entrained the rhythm; iliopsoas stretch suppressed knee extensor activity and advanced knee flexor onset.
CorrectsWhen a swinging foot hits an obstacle, skin input can trigger a phase-dependent spinal stumbling correction that lifts the foot. Once triggered, the organized response can be expressed without further feedback in experimental conditions. The cerebellum also helps fine-tune voluntary movement; that is distinct from the preorganized spinal response.
ReinforcesLoad-related proprioceptive feedback can boost extensor activity during stance. The deck quotes roughly 30–70% in experimental locomotion contexts. This conveys that feedback can contribute substantially; it is not a fixed fraction for every muscle, species, or gait.

Shared circuits and motor modules

Flexion reflex afferents (FRA) are sensory fibres capable of evoking a flexion reflex. Some of the interneurons they recruit also participate in locomotor circuits; activating this input can therefore recruit rhythmic flexor–extensor alternation under suitable conditions. A withdrawal with crossed extension and a stumble correction are examples of spinal circuits coordinating a whole response.

Other rhythm-generating networks support scratching, paw shaking, breathing and chewing. Which patterns can run together depends on the networks and tasks involved. The Rybak–McCrea model separates rhythm generation (timing) from pattern formation (which motor pools are recruited), and uses sensory inputs to reproduce experimental changes in timing and muscle output.

Quick revision

High-yield summary

Reflex arc and localisation

  • Spindle Ia = length; tendon organ Ib = tension.
  • Withdrawal = flex on the stimulated side; crossed extension = support on the other side.
  • LMN: low tone, low reflexes, denervation atrophy. Established UMN: brisk reflexes, spasticity, extensor plantar response.
  • Fasciculation = motor unit; fibrillation = single fibre.

Modulation and locomotion

  • Babinski = changed pattern; hyperreflexia = increased gain.
  • Baclofen: GABAB; less transmitter release before the synapse, lower excitability after it.
  • CPG sets a basic rhythm. Feedback retimes, corrects and reinforces.
  • Hip extension helps switch stance to swing; obstacle contact can trigger a spinal correction.
