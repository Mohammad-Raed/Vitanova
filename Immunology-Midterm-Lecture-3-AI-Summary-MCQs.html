Innate immunity: barriers and sensing

Components and cells of innate immunity, the barriers that keep microbes out, a burn-patient case, and how innate cells sense microbes.

1. Explanation2. High-yield summary3. Practice MCQs
Part 1

Explanation

Objectives of this lecture (full list)

  • Describe physical, chemical and microbial barriers at each portal of entry.
  • Explain how innate cells sense microbes and are recruited into tissues.
  • Outline phagocytosis and the mechanisms phagocytes use to kill microbes.
  • Describe inflammation, fever and the acute-phase response; interpret CRP and procalcitonin.
  • Summarise complement pathways and key innate cytokines, including drugs that target them.
  • Compare the roles of neutrophils, macrophages, eosinophils, mast cells and NK cells.

Sessions 3, 4 and 5 of this guide cover these objectives in order.

What innate immunity is made of

External barriers

Skin and other barriers, plus internal defences (cells and soluble molecules such as CRP and complement).

Cells

Phagocytes (neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages); also eosinophils, basophils, mast cells, dendritic cells, NK cells and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs).

Soluble molecules

Complement, acute-phase proteins, cytokines, antimicrobial peptides.

Timing

Ready at birth; responds in minutes to hours. Innate signals start and steer the adaptive response.
  • G-CSF (filgrastim) – granulocyte colony-stimulating factor – stimulates the bone marrow to make neutrophils and restores them after chemotherapy.
  • Where cells mature: NK cells and B cells in the bone marrow; T cells in the thymus.
  • The note-taker adds that innate cells carry germline-encoded receptors, which are fast and recognise certain microbes (for example specific viruses). Extra

Barriers: keeping microbes out

SiteDefence
SkinKeratinised layers, tight junctions, shedding, sebum fatty acids, acidic pH (about 5)
Respiratory tractMucus, cilia (mucociliary escalator), cough and sneeze reflexes
GutGastric acid (pH 1–3), bile, peristalsis, Paneth-cell defensins
Urinary / genitalUrine flushing; lactobacilli keep vaginal pH about 4
Eyes, mouthTears and saliva rich in lysozyme

When barriers are breached: burns, IV catheters, urinary catheters, intubation and surgery all open the door to infection.

Clarification: the note-taker points out that most bacteria cannot tolerate an acidic environment, yet H. pylori survives in the highly acidic stomach as an exception. Extra

Chemical and microbial barriers

  • Lysozyme (tears and saliva) digests peptidoglycan in bacterial cell walls.
  • Defensins and cathelicidin (LL-37) disrupt microbial membranes.
  • Acidic or low-pH sites (stomach, urine, vagina, skin) slow bacterial growth.
  • Mucociliary escalator traps and sweeps microbes out; impaired in cystic fibrosis and by smoking.
  • Normal microbiota occupy space, use nutrients and make bacteriocins. Antibiotics disturb it → Candida overgrowth and C. difficile colitis.
  • 2026: approved microbiota therapies (Rebyota, Vowst) prevent recurrent C. difficile.
Flag: the slide includes a small cell-wall picture labelled thick = Gram-positive, thin = Gram-negative (to illustrate where lysozyme acts). The note-taker marks one slide part as “not important”; it is not reproduced here.

Case: burns and sepsis

A man is transferred to a burn unit after a helicopter crash with multiple injuries, including third-degree burns over 65% of his body. Within 3 days he develops fever, chills, hypotension and tachycardia. Pseudomonas aeruginosa grows from the burn wound and blood cultures.

QuestionSlide answer
Which defence failed?Skin barrier lost, plus burn-induced immune suppression
Why Pseudomonas?Opportunist, forms biofilms, drug-resistant
What next, and what is the priority?Sepsis → cultures, antipseudomonal antibiotics within 1 hour, fluids, wound excision

The note-taker adds that skin flora and opportunists take advantage once the skin is damaged and then invade the body. Extra

How innate cells sense microbes

White blood cells recognise microbes using receptors. Each receptor sits where its microbe is found: on the cell surface or inside the cell. This is an important slide.

Part 2

High-yield summary

  • Innate immunity is ready at birth and acts in minutes to hours; it consists of barriers, cells (neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, DCs, mast cells, eosinophils, basophils, NK cells, ILCs) and molecules (complement, acute-phase proteins, cytokines, antimicrobial peptides).
  • G-CSF (filgrastim) restores neutrophils after chemotherapy.
  • Barriers: skin (keratin, sebum, pH ≈ 5), mucus and cilia, gastric acid (pH 1–3), urine flushing, vaginal pH ≈ 4, lysozyme in tears and saliva.
  • Lysozyme digests peptidoglycan; defensins and LL-37 disrupt membranes; mucociliary clearance is impaired in cystic fibrosis and smoking.
  • Normal flora protect; antibiotics → Candida, C. difficile.
  • Burn case: lost skin barrier → Pseudomonas sepsis → cultures, antipseudomonal antibiotics within 1 h, fluids, wound excision.
  • TLR4 = LPS; TLR2 = lipoproteins; TLR5 = flagellin; TLR3/7/8/9 = nucleic acids; RIG-I = viral RNA; cGAS–STING = DNA.
  • NLRP3 → caspase-1 → IL-1β (also urate/gout); mutations → CAPS (IL-1 blockade).
  • NF-κB → inflammatory cytokines; IRFs → type I IFN; CpG (TLR9) = adjuvant in Heplisav-B.
