Lecture 1 · Skull

Bones of the skull

8 cranial bones

Form the cranial cavity: frontal, 2 parietal, 2 temporal, occipital, sphenoid, ethmoid.

14 facial bones

Form the facial skeleton, including parts of the nasal and orbital cavities: 2 nasal, 2 maxillae, 2 zygomatic, mandible, 2 lacrimal, 2 palatine, 2 inferior conchae, vomer.
Reading the nerve labels: CN means cranial nerve; Roman numerals identify the nerve. The trigeminal nerve is CN V: its maxillary division is V2 and its mandibular division is V3.
Cavities to know: cranialnasalorbitalear The frontal orbital plates separate the anterior cranial fossa from the orbits. The ethmoid cribriform plate separates the anterior cranial fossa from the nasal cavity; these are neighbouring spaces, not one continuous cavity.

Sutures and landmarks

Coronal frontal + both parietals
Sagittal two parietals
Lambdoid parietals + occipital
Squamous parietal + temporal
Bregma: coronal meets sagittal. Lambda: sagittal meets lambdoid. Pterion: lateral junction of frontal, parietal, sphenoid, temporal. Orbitomeatal plane: orients the skull in anatomical position (parallel to the ground).
Clinical link: The pterion is a thin, clinically vulnerable region of the lateral skull. The middle meningeal artery (enters via foramen spinosum) lies just deep to it, so a fracture here can cause an extradural haematoma.

Lab link: Foramen lacerum lies between the petrous apex and sphenoid; in life it is largely filled with fibrocartilage. The internal carotid artery passes over its superior aspect, not vertically through the cartilage-filled opening. Stylomastoid foramen lies between the styloid and mastoid processes: CN VII exits the temporal bone here after entering the internal acoustic meatus and traversing the facial canal.

The ear cavity (petrous temporal)

  • External acoustic meatus: the external ear canal ends at the tympanic membrane, which normally separates it from the middle ear.
  • Tympanic cavity (middle ear): contains the auditory ossicles and communicates with the nasopharynx through the pharyngotympanic (auditory or Eustachian) tube.
  • Internal acoustic meatus: carries CN VII and CN VIII from the posterior cranial fossa into the petrous temporal bone. It is a neurovascular passage, not an open connection between the middle ear and the cranial cavity.

  • Superior orbital fissure: between lesser and greater wings; connects orbit to the middle cranial fossa.
  • Inferior orbital fissure: between the greater wing of the sphenoid and the maxilla; communicates medially with the pterygopalatine fossa and laterally with the infratemporal fossa.

Pterygopalatine fossa (PPF)

Boundaries: anteriorly, the posterior surface of the maxilla; posteriorly, the pterygoid process and adjacent sphenoid; medially, the perpendicular plate of the palatine bone. It opens laterally through the pterygomaxillary fissure, superiorly toward the inferior orbital fissure, and inferiorly into the palatine canals. Contents: maxillary nerve (V2), pterygopalatine ganglion, and the terminal part and branches of the maxillary artery. Preganglionic parasympathetic fibres synapse in the ganglion; sensory fibres of V2 pass through without synapsing.

Lab link: The maxillary nerve (V2) enters the PPF through foramen rotundum. Its infra-orbital continuation then passes through the inferior orbital fissure → infra-orbital groove and canal → infra-orbital foramen. Superior alveolar branches supply the upper teeth; the terminal infra-orbital branches supply the lower eyelid, side of the nose and upper lip.

High-yield summary

  • 22 bones = 8 cranial + 14 facial.
  • Four sutures: coronal, sagittal, lambdoid, squamous. Pterion = weak point over the middle meningeal artery.
  • Sphenoid: wings, sella turcica, pterygoids, foramina rotundum, ovale, spinosum.
  • Ethmoid: cribriform plate, crista galli, perpendicular plate.
  • Petrous temporal: ear, internal acoustic meatus, carotid canal.
  • PPF is the junction box linking orbit, nose, mouth, pharynx and cranium.
Developmental-age note: the metopic-suture quiz retains the supplied lecture’s approximate “by age 6” wording. This should not be read as a universal fusion age: closure timing varies and persistence can be a normal variant.

Lecture 2 · Neonatal skull

Neonatal versus adult skull

Key differences

  • Large cranium relative to face (face grows through childhood).
  • The mastoid process is not yet developed at birth; it becomes prominent after birth.
  • Mandible: two halves fuse at the mandibular symphysis, usually during the first 1–2 years; angle is obtuse.
  • Bones separated by unossified membranes: fontanelles.

Ossification

Intramembranous most flat bones: mesenchyme → ossification centres; leftover membranes become fontanelles and then sutures.

Endochondral passes through a hyaline cartilage model first.

Lecture 2 · TMJ

Temporomandibular joint

  • A paired synovial joint between the mandible and the temporal bone, combining hinge and gliding movements. It is the main movable articulation of the facial skeleton; do not interpret this as excluding the small synovial joints between the auditory ossicles.
  • Surfaces: head (condyle) of mandible with mandibular fossa and articular tubercle of temporal bone.
  • Articular disc (fibrocartilage) attached to capsule and head of mandible; divides joint in two.
  • Nerve supply: auriculotemporal and masseteric branches of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (V3).
Upper compartment: gliding (translation)·Lower compartment: hinge (rotation)

Early mouth opening mainly involves rotation in the lower compartment. Wider opening adds anterior translation of the condyle–disc complex in the upper compartment; the movements overlap rather than occurring as two completely separate steps.
