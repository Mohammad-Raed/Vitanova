T-cell receptor (TCR) signalling: receptor to nucleus

Starting point. An antigen-presenting cell (APC) holds a peptide in an MHC (major histocompatibility complex) molecule and shows it to the T cell. The T cell recognises it through the TCR (α and β chains). The TCR itself has no signalling tail, so the signal is carried inward by CD3, which carries ITAMs (immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs). CD3 is present on all T cells. CD4 marks helper T cells (MHC II); CD8 marks cytotoxic T cells (MHC I). Which one is used depends on the MHC class presenting the antigen.

TCR + peptide–MHC → Lck (on CD4/CD8) → ITAM phosphorylation → ZAP-70 → LAT/SLP-76 scaffold → PLCγ1 + RAS ├─ ① Ca²⁺ → calcineurin → NFAT ├─ ② DAG → PKCθ → NF-κB └─ ③ RAS → RAF → MEK → ERK → AP-1 (Fos + Jun) Net result: IL-2 gene, proliferation, differentiation
  • Lck is a tyrosine kinase (adds phosphate groups to tyrosine). It is attached to the cytoplasmic part of CD4/CD8. When the TCR binds antigen, CD4/CD8 are pulled into the contact area with the APC, bringing Lck close to CD3 so it can phosphorylate the ITAMs.
  • Immunological synapse: the T cell–APC contact zone. TCRs cluster in the centre, adhesion molecules (LFA-1) form a ring, and contact lasts hours.
  • ZAP-70 docks on phosphorylated ITAMs, is activated, then phosphorylates LAT and SLP-76 (scaffold proteins that assemble the next enzymes). ZAP-70 defect → SCID (severe combined immunodeficiency), with CD4 counts that look normal.
  • Three arms (all three needed, since each activates a different transcription factor):
    ① Calcium arm: PLCγ1 cleaves PIP₂ into IP₃ and DAG. IP₃ releases Ca²⁺ from intracellular stores; raised Ca²⁺ activates calmodulin, then the phosphatase calcineurin, which removes phosphate from NFAT so it can enter the nucleus and switch on the IL-2 gene.
    ② DAG arm: DAG activates PKCθ, leading to NF-κB activation and nuclear entry.
    ③ RAS arm: RAS → RAF → MEK → ERK → AP-1 (made of Fos and Jun), which enters the nucleus.
  • Anergy: TCR signalling without costimulation gives NFAT alone, so the cell becomes functionally unresponsive instead of activated.
  • Drugs: tacrolimus and ciclosporin block calcineurin; checkpoint molecules recruit phosphatases to switch the cascade off.
Abbreviations of enzymes and receptors must be memorised. Highlighted: Lck carried by CD4/CD8; ITAM phosphorylation → ZAP-70. The three arms are numbered 1–3; the calcium arm depends on calcium. ZAP-70 defect → SCID. Drug and clinical lines flagged. Boxed: IL-2, proliferation, differentiation = net result.

B-cell receptor (BCR) signalling

The BCR is membrane immunoglobulin (IgM, IgD) with two antigen-binding sites. Its signalling partners are Igα/Igβ (CD79a/b), which carry ITAMs, equivalent to CD3 in T cells. The pathway is almost the same as in T cells with different names.

BCR → Lyn → Syk → BLNK + BTK (+ PLCγ2) → PIP₂ → IP₃ + DAG IP₃ → Ca²⁺ → NFAT DAG → PKCβ → NF-κB → nucleus: new genes (survival, proliferation, differentiation)
  • Cross-linking: antigen must bind two or more receptors to begin signalling (hence two binding sites).
  • Phosphorylation: Lyn phosphorylates ITAMs of Igα/Igβ; Syk docks and is activated.
  • Assembly: BLNK is a scaffold protein that gathers BTK and PLCγ2.
  • Second messengers: PLCγ2 splits PIP₂; calcium activates NFAT; DAG activates PKCβ then NF-κB.
  • Outcome: new gene transcription.

Amplifier: the CD19/CD21 co-receptor acts through PI3K and lowers the threshold, so a weaker antigen signal still activates the cell. Clinical: BTK inhibitors (e.g. ibrutinib) treat CLL (chronic lymphocytic leukaemia); BTK loss causes XLA (X-linked agammaglobulinaemia).

Clinical points are usually tested as cases. CD19/CD21 amplifier: the cell needs a weaker signal to activate. Correction: the figure draws ibrutinib beside Syk/BLNK–BTK, but it inhibits BTK.

IL-2: the proliferation signal

IL-2 is the major growth factor for activated T cells. Signals 1 + 2 (TCR signal + costimulation) work through NFAT, NF-κB and AP-1 to switch on the IL-2 gene.

  • High-affinity receptor has 3 subunits: CD25 (α), β (CD122) and the common γ chain (CD132). Subunit numbers must be memorised
  • Autocrine loop: the activated clone makes IL-2 and responds to it, dividing about every 6–8 hours Emphasised
  • Tregs (regulatory T cells) are CD25-high; they sustain themselves on IL-2 and compete for it, acting as an IL-2 sink that restrains responses. Annotation: IL-2 switches the response on and, through Tregs, off.
  • Drugs: tacrolimus / ciclosporin (calcineurin block, so no IL-2 made; famous drugs), basiliximab (anti-CD25), sirolimus (blocks mTOR).
  • Genetics: γ-chain loss → X-linked SCID; CD25 deficiency → IPEX-like autoimmunity (immune dysregulation, polyendocrinopathy, enteropathy, X-linked; FOXP3 defect is the classic cause).

What an activated lymphocyte looks like

  • Morphology: small resting lymphocyte → large blast with prominent nucleoli and more cytoplasm.
  • Division: one cell can give thousands of daughters within days. Result of IL-2 production (annotation reads “IL-12”; corrected to IL-2)
  • Surface markers (in order): CD69 within hours → CD25 by 1–2 days (annotation: α chain of IL-2R) → CD71 while dividing (figure: 2–3 d) → HLA-DR by 3–5 days.
  • Metabolism: oxidative phosphorylation → aerobic glycolysis to fuel growth; seen as uptake on PET scans.
  • Contraction: ~90% of effector cells die by activation-induced cell death (AICD), leaving memory cells.
  • Historical: the lymphocyte transformation test used mitogens (PHA) to show T cells could respond.

Becoming a plasma cell

Activated B cell → plasmablast → plasma cell is a one-way, terminal change.

  • Transcription switch: PAX5 and BCL6 OFF; BLIMP-1 and XBP1 ON (B-cell identity off, secretory machinery on).
  • Huge rough endoplasmic reticulum, eccentric “clock-face” nucleus. Thousands of antibody molecules per second.
  • Markers: surface Ig and CD20 are lost; CD138 appears.
  • Clinical: rituximab (anti-CD20) spares plasma cells, so IgG levels often persist. Long-lived plasma cells sit in bone-marrow niches; myeloma is their malignancy.

TypeActivatorMechanism / cells
MonoclonalConventional antigenProcessed peptide in the MHC groove activates one rare clone (1 in 10⁴–10⁶ T cells)
OligoclonalSuperantigenBinds MHC II and TCR Vβ outside the groove, unprocessed → up to 20% of T cells
PolyclonalMitogensPHA and concanavalin A (T cells), LPS (B cells), pokeweed (both)
  • Examples: staphylococcal TSST-1 (toxic shock syndrome toxin-1) and enterotoxins; streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins SpeA, SpeC. Annotation: they trigger release of very large amounts of cytokines.
  • Mechanism: the toxin cross-links the variable β region of the TCR to the α chain of MHC II, so any T-cell clone with that Vβ is activated, without specific peptide recognition. T cells release IFN-γ → macrophages release IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α → systemic toxicity.
  • Result: cytokine storm (TNF, IL-2, IFN-γ) → fever, rash, capillary leak, shock; later anergy and deletion.
  • Mitogen uses: lymphocyte function tests and karyotyping (metaphase spreads).
Regular antigen: specific response, just enough to clear the antigen. Superantigen: non-specific and excessive. Marked: oligoclonal; conventional antigen vs superantigen.

Case: teenager with fever, rash and shock

A 16-year-old on day 3 of her period (tampon use): fever 39.8 °C, vomiting, diarrhoea, sunburn-like erythroderma, BP 78/40 mmHg, confusion, raised creatinine and liver enzymes, platelets 85 × 10⁹/L, negative blood cultures, vaginal swab grows Staphylococcus aureus; later desquamation of palms and soles.

  • Recognise toxic shock syndrome: fever, rash, hypotension, multi-organ involvement.
  • Mechanism: TSST-1 cross-links MHC II and TCR Vβ → up to 20% of T cells release cytokines.
  • Cultures negative because disease is toxin-mediated, not bacteraemic.
  • Treat: remove the source, fluids and vasopressors, anti-staphylococcal antibiotic plus clindamycin (stops toxin synthesis); consider IVIG (intravenous immunoglobulin).

High-yield revision summary

  • TCR: Lck (CD4/CD8) → ITAM → ZAP-70 → LAT/SLP-76 → NFAT (Ca²⁺/calcineurin), NF-κB (DAG/PKCθ), AP-1 (RAS/ERK). Signal 1 alone = anergy. ZAP-70 defect = SCID.
  • BCR: cross-linking → Lyn → Syk → BLNK + BTK + PLCγ2 → NFAT, NF-κB. CD19/CD21 → PI3K lowers threshold. BTK: XLA (loss), CLL (inhibitors).
  • IL-2: NFAT + NF-κB + AP-1; receptor CD25 + CD122 + CD132; autocrine, divides every 6–8 h; Treg sink. Tacrolimus/ciclosporin, basiliximab, sirolimus. γc loss = X-SCID.
  • Activated cell: blast; CD69 → CD25 → CD71 → HLA-DR; glycolysis; ~90% die (AICD).
  • Plasma cell: PAX5/BCL6 off, BLIMP-1/XBP1 on; CD20 lost, CD138 gained; rituximab spares; myeloma.
  • Superantigen: binds MHC II + Vβ outside groove, ≤20% of T cells, cytokine storm, TSS; cultures negative; clindamycin ± IVIG.

Factors that decide whether a response happens

Key exam question: why does one antigen produce a strong response in one person but a weak or no response in another? Answered by the seven highlighted factors below.
FactorExplanation
Immunogen vs antigenAn immunogen provokes a response; an antigen is anything that can be bound. Every immunogen is an antigen, but not every antigen is an immunogen.
Antigen factors (A–D)(A) size, usually >10 kDa; (B) chemical complexity; (C) degradability; (D) foreignness. Proteins are the best immunogens because only they yield peptides for T cells.
Dose ★Too little is ignored; too much can tolerise. Optimal dose lies between (therapeutic window). Curve: very low → tolerance/ignored; optimal → strong response; very high → tolerance.
RouteIntradermal and subcutaneous most immunogenic (many dendritic cells); intramuscular standard for most vaccines; oral usually favours tolerance (or mucosal IgA); intravenous reaches spleen and can tolerise.
AgeNeonates and elderly respond less well, hence extra doses and adjuvanted vaccines for older adults.
GeneticsHLA type decides which peptides can be presented at all, so responses differ between people.
General healthMalnutrition, immunosuppressive drugs, malignancy and chronic infection reduce responses.

Extra The slide’s figure groups these as four headings: age, genes, health, the antigen.

High-yield revision summary

  • Immunogen ⊂ antigen. Best immunogens: large (>10 kDa), complex, degradable, foreign proteins.
  • Dose is bell-shaped: low and high doses tolerise. Intradermal/subcutaneous strongest; oral → tolerance.
  • Extremes of age, poor health, immunosuppression, and HLA type change the response.
