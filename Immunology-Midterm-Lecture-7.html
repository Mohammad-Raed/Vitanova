Lecture 7 — Organisation of the lymphatic system, lymph, primary organs, recirculation and lymph node

Objectives: distinguish primary from secondary lymphoid organs; describe lymph formation and drainage; explain the structure of bone marrow, thymus and lymph node in relation to function; describe lymphocyte recirculation and tissue-specific homing.

1. How the lymphatic system is organised

Purpose. Only about 1 in 10⁵–10⁶ lymphocytes can recognise a given antigen. The lymphatic system brings these rare antigen-specific lymphocytes together with antigen and antigen-presenting cells (APCs).

Primary (generative)Secondary (peripheral)
MembersBone marrow, thymusLymph nodes, spleen, MALT, skin-associated tissue
FunctionLymphopoiesis: lymphocytes are produced and mature before any antigen is metSites of antigen exposure, where immune responses start
Filter role (note)—Spleen = blood filter; lymph nodes = tissue (lymph) filter; MALT = monitors mucosal surfaces

Maturation sites: B cell → bone marrow; T cell → thymus.

Traffic. Mature cells leave the primary organs and recirculate continuously between blood and secondary organs. A naive lymphocyte that does not meet its specific antigen continues to recirculate between blood and secondary lymphoid organs until it does.

Origin of the names. Removal of the bursa of Fabricius from chickens (Ohio State, 1950s) abolished antibody production, giving "B" cells. Removal of the newborn thymus in mice (Miller, 1961) abolished cellular immunity, giving "T" cells. Humans have no bursa; the bone marrow serves that role.

2. Lymph: formation, vessels and drainage

Blood capillaries→Interstitial fluid→Initial lymphatic capillaries→Collecting lymphatic vessels→Lymph nodes→Lymphatic trunks→Lymphatic ducts→Subclavian veins→Blood
  • Formation: lymph starts as interstitial fluid filtered from blood capillaries into tissue.
  • Initial lymphatics are blind-ended, with overlapping endothelial flaps and anchoring filaments. Fluid enters but cannot leak back.
  • One-way flow is produced by four mechanisms (numbered 1–4 on the slide): valves, skeletal muscle contraction, breathing, and pumping of the vessels themselves.
  • Compulsory checkpoint: lymph passes through at least one node before returning to the blood.
  • Return to circulation by two ducts: (1) the thoracic duct, draining most of the body, and (2) the right lymphatic duct. Both empty into the subclavian veins. About 2–3 litres of lymph return per day.
  • Lacteals (lymphatic capillaries in intestinal villi) absorb dietary fat as chyle.
  • Clinical: thoracic duct injury → chylothorax. Chyle leaks into the pleural cavity (handwritten clarification). Lymph also carries tumour cells to regional nodes.

3. Bone marrow (primary organ)

  • About 10¹¹ blood cells are produced daily from haematopoietic stem cells.
  • Antigen-independent generation: lymphocytes are produced before meeting any antigen. A handwritten note explains that bone marrow is a primary organ because this development is a normal, antigen-independent process.
  • B cells mature here. T-cell precursors leave for the thymus.
  • Stromal niches supply CXCL12 (a chemokine) and IL-7 (a cytokine), plus the cell contact needed for development.
  • Survival niche: after B-cell activation, some cells become long-lived plasma cells, migrate to the marrow, receive survival signals and continue to secrete antibody for years. This maintains antibody levels long after infection or vaccination has ended (emphasised by the lecturer).
  • Adult red marrow: sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis, skull, proximal femora and humeri. Elsewhere the marrow is fatty yellow marrow.
  • Clinical: marrow biopsy is taken from the posterior iliac crest and is used to diagnose some types of leukaemia and lymphoma. G-CSF mobilises stem cells for transplantation. Marrow failure → pancytopenia.

4. Thymus (primary organ)

  • Position: bilobed organ in the anterior mediastinum, behind the sternum.
  • Epithelial cells provide the MHC and self-antigens used for selection (Lecture 5 topic).
  • Life cycle: 10–15 g at birth, ~35 g at puberty, then fatty involution; output continues for life.
  • Output measure: TRECs quantify new T cells and are used in newborn screening for SCID.
  • Clinical: thymoma is associated with myasthenia gravis; thymic hypoplasia occurs in 22q11.2 deletion (DiGeorge syndrome).

CortexDensely packed immature thymocytes and cortical epithelial cells; protected by a blood–thymus barrier

ClarificationThymoma → impaired immune tolerance → activation of autoreactive T cells → B-cell activation → autoantibodies against acetylcholine receptors (AChR) → myasthenia gravis.

Additional reading on T-cell selection (slide marked "extra, read only")

  • Immature T-lineage cells leave the marrow for the thymus, the second primary lymphoid organ, which is located above the heart: outer cortex of immature T cells, inner medulla into which cells pass as they mature.
  • Developing thymocytes expressing a T-cell receptor (TCR) undergo a two-step selection. The TCR binds antigenic peptides presented by APCs, so cells that would bind normal self-antigens (causing autoimmunity) and cells with no attraction to APC surfaces must be removed.
  • Selection depends on exposure to high levels of MHC antigens.
  • Cells that complete selection are mature naive lymphocytes that begin recirculation, so the small number of cells with receptors for a given antigen can search the whole body. Naive cells preferentially recirculate through lymph nodes, spleen and MALT.

5. Lymphocyte recirculation and homing

  • Constant patrol: naive lymphocytes cycle between blood, secondary organs and lymph every 12–24 hours.
  • Route: blood → high endothelial venules (HEVs) → node → efferent lymph → thoracic duct → blood again.
  • Only about 2% of lymphocytes are in the blood at any moment; the rest are in tissues and organs.
  • Effector cells change address: activation switches their receptors so they home to the infected tissue.
  • Drug link: vedolizumab blocks α4β7 integrin and acts selectively on the gut in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).

7. What happens in a node during a response

ClarificationRoute of antigen:
Infected tissue→Antigen uptake by dendritic cells→Afferent lymphatic vessels→Lymph node
  1. Antigen arrives free in lymph, or carried by dendritic cells from the infected tissue.
  2. Trapping: matching lymphocytes are briefly retained instead of leaving.
  3. Activation and proliferation: germinal centres form and the node enlarges (lymphadenopathy).
  4. Outputs: effector T cells leave for the infected tissue; plasma cells stay and export antibody into blood; memory cells are generated.
  5. Resolution: a reactive node is tender, mobile, usually <2 cm, and settles over weeks.

High-yield revision summary — Lecture 7

  • Primary (bone marrow, thymus) = lymphocyte development before antigen; secondary (nodes, spleen, MALT) = antigen exposure. B matures in marrow; T in thymus.
  • Blood filter = spleen; tissue filter = lymph node; mucosal monitor = MALT.
  • Production of lymphocytes is regulated (up in infection, via cytokines). Raised CBC lymphocytes usually mean viral infection. B/T counts need flow cytometry.
  • Lymph path: blood capillary → interstitial fluid → initial lymphatics → collecting vessels → nodes → trunks → ducts → subclavian veins. 2–3 L/day. Flow: valves, muscle, breathing, vessel pumping.
  • Thoracic duct injury → chylothorax. Lacteals absorb fat as chyle.
  • Marrow: stromal niche = CXCL12 (chemokine) + IL-7 (cytokine); long-lived plasma cells live here; biopsy at posterior iliac crest (leukaemia/lymphoma diagnosis).
  • Thymus: cortex = immature, medulla = mature + Hassall's corpuscles; peak ~35 g at puberty; TRECs/SCID screening; thymoma → myasthenia gravis; 22q11.2 deletion → hypoplasia.
  • Homing: node = L-selectin + CCR7; gut = α4β7 + CCR9; skin = CLA + CCR4. Vedolizumab blocks α4β7 (IBD). Effectors change their homing receptors.
  • Node: cortex = B follicles; paracortex = T cells + DCs + HEVs; medulla = plasma cells/cords; one efferent vessel at hilum; small antigens use conduits (<70 kDa).
  • Reactive node: tender, mobile, <2 cm, resolves in weeks.
