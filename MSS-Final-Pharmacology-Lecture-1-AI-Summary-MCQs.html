Pharmacology

Acne vulgaris and its treatment

Pathogenesis

  • Chronic inflammatory disease of the pilosebaceous unit; very common (~80% of people), real psychological impact.
  • Four factors: ↑ sebum (androgen-driven), follicular plugging (hyperkeratinisation → comedone), bacteria, inflammation.
  • C. acnes: anaerobic Gram-positive; thrives in plugged follicles, makes free fatty acids → inflammation.
  • Other causes (lecture notes): PCOS, drug-induced (corticosteroids, anti-TB drugs, vitamin B12/multivitamins).

Not acne vulgaris

Rosacea: flushing, visible vessels, pus-filled bumps, no comedones; flares for weeks–months. Treated with metronidazole. Acne has no cure: treatment aims to control it according to severity.

Stepwise strategy

Comedonal: retinoid / salicylic / azelaic→Mild: topical combination→Moderate: topical + oral antibiotic→Severe, nodular or resistant: isotretinoin
Hormonal acne in females: COC and/or spironolactone at any stage, alongside other treatments.

Clarifications: (1) The slide titled “Isotretinoin mechanism” actually describes tretinoin (all-trans retinoic acid, the topical form); isotretinoin is the 13-cis oral form. (2) “0.025–0.5%” belongs to topical retinoids, not azelaic acid (the slide merges them). (3) Marked sebum reduction is mainly an oral isotretinoin effect; topical retinoids mainly target keratinisation.
Mnemonics: Retinoid = Keratin · BPO = Bacteria · Azelaic = All-rounder (anti-inflammatory, anti-androgenic, antibacterial) · Spironolactone = Sebum via androgens. “Clinda/Erythro never alone.” Retinoids at night, BPO not at night.
Last-minute

High-yield summary

  • Comedones → retinoid; mild/moderate inflammatory → topical antibacterial (not alone); severe → isotretinoin (teratogenic).
  • Rosacea → metronidazole.
