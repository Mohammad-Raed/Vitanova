Lecture 5

Bones of the upper limb

Clavicle + Scapula (girdle)→Humerus (arm)→Radius + Ulna→8 carpals · 5 metacarpals · 14 phalanges

Clavicle

Features
  • S-shaped, long, slender; curves add resilience
  • Palpable across the root of the neck
  • A strut holding the limb away from the trunk
  • The only bony attachment of the upper limb to the trunk
Ends and surfaces
  • Sternal end (triangular, medial): manubrium + 1st costal cartilage
  • Acromial end (flat, lateral): acromion
  • Anteriorly: medial 2/3 convex, lateral 1/3 concave
  • Superior surface smooth; inferior rough
Inferior-surface landmarks
  • Conoid tubercle: conoid ligament (medial part of coracoclavicular)
  • Trapezoid line: trapezoid ligament (lateral part)
  • Subclavian groove: subclavius
  • Impression for costoclavicular ligament
Clinical link: the clavicle is commonly fractured, most often through its middle third, including near the junction of the medial two thirds and lateral third. Avoid treating it as the most commonly fractured bone in every age group or population.

Scapula

  • Triangular flat bone, posterior chest wall, between ribs 2 and 7
  • 3 angles (inferior, superior, lateral/glenoid); 3 borders (superior, medial/vertebral, lateral/axillary); 2 surfaces (costal with subscapular fossa; posterior with spine); 3 processes (acromion, spine, coracoid)
  • Supraglenoid tubercle: long head of biceps. Infraglenoid tubercle: long head of triceps
  • Suprascapular notch: suprascapular nerve passes below the transverse scapular ligament (artery above)

Humerus

PartKey features
Proximal endHead (1/3 sphere); anatomical neck (capsule attachment); lesser tubercle (anterior); greater tubercle (lateral); intertubercular sulcus (long head of biceps)
Sulcus attachmentsLateral lip: pectoralis major; floor: latissimus dorsi; medial lip: teres major (“Lady between two Majors”)

ShaftDeltoid tuberosity (lateral, V-shaped); radial (spiral) groove: radial nerve + profunda brachii artery

Forearm and hand

Radius
  • Lateral, shorter; shaft convex laterally, wider below
  • Articulates: capitulum, ulna (proximal and distal radioulnar joints), scaphoid and lunate
  • Radial tuberosity, styloid process, ulnar notch
Ulna
  • Medial, longer, the stabilizing bone; wider above
  • Olecranon, coronoid, trochlear notch, radial notch
  • Does not articulate directly with the carpal bones at the radiocarpal joint; an articular disc separates the ulnar head from the proximal carpal row
  • Posterior border sharp, palpable along its length
Interosseous membrane
  • Fibres run obliquely: down from radius, medially to ulna
  • Shares load between the forearm bones: force received by the radius can pass through the membrane to the ulna, then to the humerus. It is a load-sharing pathway, not the only path for every force
Carpals (lateral → medial)
  • Proximal: ScaphoidLunateTriquetrumPisiform
  • Distal: TrapeziumTrapezoidCapitateHamate
  • “Stop Letting Those People Touch The Cadaver’s Hand”
  • Pisiform = sesamoid in flexor carpi ulnaris tendon; capitate is the largest (metacarpal III); trapezium meets the thumb metacarpal; hamate has a hook
  • Carpal arch: lateral = scaphoid + trapezium tubercles; medial = pisiform + hook of hamate; flexor retinaculum spans them → carpal tunnel
  • Metacarpals: base, shaft, head (long bones). Phalanges: proximal, middle, distal; thumb has only two
Clinical link: scaphoid is the most commonly fractured carpal bone, often across its waist; this does not mean it causes most fractures around the wrist. Much of its blood supply reaches the proximal pole in a retrograde direction, making that pole vulnerable to non-union and avascular necrosis (AVN) after some fractures. These complications are risks, not inevitable outcomes.
Theory meets lab

Lecture 5: what to find on the bones

On the bone

  • Identify clavicle side: sternal end triangular, conoid tubercle posterior-inferior at the lateral end
  • Scapula: locate both tubercles, suprascapular notch, subscapular fossa
  • Humerus: trace the radial groove (posterior, below deltoid tuberosity)
  • Distinguish radius vs ulna: ulna has olecranon and trochlear notch; radius has a proximal disc-shaped head and a distal lateral styloid; the ulna also has a head and styloid, both at its distal end

On the living body

  • Palpable: clavicle, spine and acromion of scapula, coracoid tip, inferior angle (approximate resting level of rib 7 and the seventh thoracic vertebral (T7) spinous process; position changes with posture and arm movement), medial epicondyle, olecranon, ulna’s posterior border
  • Ulnar nerve rolls against the posterior medial epicondyle

On X-ray

  • Shoulder: clavicle, acromion, coracoid, humeral head, glenoid
  • Elbow: capitulum, trochlea, epicondyles, radial head, olecranon, coronoid
  • Hand: 8 carpals, 5 metacarpals, phalanges (thumb has 2)
Lecture 6

Joints of the upper limb

Lecture scope: this section covers the sternoclavicular (SC), acromioclavicular (AC) and glenohumeral joints. Relations and ligaments are emphasised in the supplied course notes; Course note identifies that emphasis, not a separate anatomical classification.

Synovial joint basics

  • A joint cavity permits movement; most synovial articular surfaces have hyaline cartilage. Important exceptions include the SC and AC joints and the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), whose surfaces have fibrocartilaginous covering
  • Course note Joint capsule = an outer fibrous layer with an inner synovial membrane. The membrane lines non-articular internal surfaces, not the articular cartilage; synovial fluid lubricates the joint
  • Additional structures vary: a disc divides the SC joint; the AC disc may be incomplete or absent; the knee has menisci rather than one complete articular disc. Ligaments and fat pads also differ between joints. Course note Not all joints share the same structures

Sternoclavicular (SC) joint

Basics
  • Sternal end of clavicle + clavicular notch of manubrium + small part of 1st costal cartilage
  • Easily palpated (clavicle sits above manubrium)
  • A saddle-type synovial joint that permits movement in several planes; “functions as ball-and-socket” describes its movement range, not its articular shape
  • Capsule thin inferiorly, otherwise reinforced by ligaments
  • Movements: elevation, depression, protraction, retraction, rotation
Articular disc
  • Fibrocartilage; splits joint into two compartments
  • Superior: medial clavicle; inferior: sternum / 1st costal cartilage junction
  • Course note Prevents medial displacement (dislocation) of the clavicle; shock absorber
Ligaments ★
  • Anterior and posterior sternoclavicular; interclavicular (reinforces the superior capsule)
  • Costoclavicular (accessory): extracapsular, from 1st rib/costal cartilage to inferior medial clavicle; limits elevation
  • Course note Costoclavicular = the strongest ligament
Relations (marked MOST important): Anterior: skin, sternocleidomastoid, pectoralis major. Posterior: brachiocephalic vessels and deeper root-of-neck structures; the exact relation differs between the right and left joints. The left brachiocephalic vein crosses behind the manubrial region. Clinical ★: anterior dislocation = medial clavicle is displaced anteriorly (sternocleidomastoid, SCM, pull may contribute). Posterior dislocation (direct trauma) can compress trachea, oesophagus, great vessels, so it is a Course note medical emergency. Blood: internal thoracic and suprascapular arteries; nerves: supraclavicular and nerve to subclavius.

Acromioclavicular (AC) joint

Basics
  • Lateral clavicle facet + acromion facet
  • Synovial plane joint; gliding in anteroposterior direction
  • An intra-articular disc may be incomplete or absent; its form varies with age and individual anatomy
  • Relations: deltoid anterior, trapezius posterior, skin superior
  • Nerves: supraclavicular, lateral pectoral, axillary
Ligaments ★
  • Acromioclavicular ligaments
  • Coracoclavicular (conoid + trapezoid): the strongest binding clavicle to scapula; coracoid process to clavicle’s undersurface
  • Course note A major vertical stabiliser: it helps suspend the scapula and upper limb from the clavicle and limits inferior displacement of the scapula relative to the clavicle
Shoulder separation ★: injury of the AC joint’s stabilising ligaments. A mild injury may involve the AC ligaments alone; a more severe separation also disrupts the coracoclavicular ligaments. The scapula and arm can drop relative to the clavicle, making the lateral clavicle appear raised.

Glenohumeral (shoulder) joint ★

Basics
  • Spheroidal humeral head + shallow, pear-shaped glenoid fossa, deepened by the glenoid labrum
  • Synovial ball-and-socket; wide movement
  • Course note Mobility is gained at the expense of stability; stability depends on ligaments and rotator cuff
Capsule
  • Thin and loose; medially outside the labrum, laterally the anatomical neck
  • Inferior part weakest: lax in adduction, taut in abduction
Synovial membrane and bursae
  • Folds around the long head of biceps tendon in the intertubercular sulcus
  • Subtendinous bursa of subscapularis: communicates with the joint; protects the subscapularis tendon
  • Subacromial bursa: protects supraspinatus tendon; bursitis causes pain on abduction and lying on that side (overuse, trauma)
LigamentAttachment / role
Glenohumeral (superior, middle, inferior)Capsular thickenings connecting the glenoid rim/labrum to the proximal humerus. Their attachments differ; the inferior complex is particularly important in stabilising the abducted shoulder
Transverse humeralGreater → lesser tubercle; bridges the intertubercular groove
CoracohumeralRoot of coracoid → greater tubercle; strengthens the superior capsule
Coracoacromial (accessory)Coracoid → acromion; protects the superior aspect
Movements and muscles
  • Rotator cuff: SITS is a mnemonic for supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis
  • Abduction: supraspinatus helps initiate the first approximately 15°, and deltoid is a major abductor thereafter. Upward scapular rotation by trapezius and serratus anterior progressively contributes and is essential for full overhead elevation; the phases overlap
  • Lateral rotation: infraspinatus, teres minor; medial rotation: subscapularis, latissimus dorsi, teres major
SideRelations ★ (mnemonic: Sara always brings snacks…)
FrontSubscapularis, axillary vessels, brachial plexus
BackInfraspinatus, teres minor
TopSupraspinatus, deltoid, subacromial bursa, coracoacromial ligament
BottomLong head of triceps, axillary nerve, posterior circumflex humeral vessels
Supply: anterior + posterior circumflex humeral arteries (axillary) and suprascapular artery (subclavian). Nerves: suprascapular, axillary, lateral pectoral. Dislocation: anterior-inferior is the usual type (head driven infero-anteriorly); posterior dislocation is less common and may follow seizures, electrical injury or trauma. Loss of the normal rounded contour is a general dislocation sign, not a defining sign of posterior dislocation.
Theory meets lab

Lecture 6: joints in the lab

Palpate and demonstrate

  • SC joint: clavicle’s medial end sits above manubrium
  • AC joint: step between lateral clavicle and acromion (site of separation)
  • Shoulder movements: abduction (supraspinatus then deltoid), rotations

On the specimen

  • Cut section of SC: disc dividing two cavities; find costoclavicular ligament
  • Coracoclavicular (conoid and trapezoid) between coracoid and clavicle
  • Shoulder: reflect muscles and name the four cuff tendons around the capsule; locate subacromial bursa and the long head of biceps in its sulcus

Bones ↔ joints

  • Anatomical neck = capsule attachment; surgical neck = axillary nerve (Lecture 5)
  • Supraglenoid tubercle/labrum = origin of the long head of biceps; its proximal tendon is intracapsular but extrasynovial
  • Clavicle’s conoid tubercle and trapezoid line = coracoclavicular ligament
  • Posterior SC relations = great vessels of the root of the neck
Last-minute review

High-yield summary

  • Clavicle: only bony link from the upper limb to the axial skeleton; commonly fractures in the middle third.
  • Inferior angle of scapula: approximate rib 7 / T7 landmark with the arm resting; posture changes the level. Supraglenoid = biceps; infraglenoid = triceps.
  • Surgical neck → axillary nerve. Radial groove → radial nerve + profunda brachii. Medial epicondyle → ulnar nerve.
  • Radius lateral and short (wrist joint); ulna medial and long (stabiliser; no direct radiocarpal articulation).
  • Scaphoid: most fractured carpal, risk of AVN (avascular necrosis). Thumb has 2 phalanges.
  • SC: saddle but acts as ball-and-socket; disc stops medial displacement; costoclavicular = strongest; posterior dislocation = emergency.
  • AC: plane joint; coracoclavicular ligament holds the limb’s weight; higher-grade AC separation can involve its rupture.
  • Shoulder: mobility over stability, weakest inferiorly, rotator cuff stabilizes, anterior-inferior dislocation commonest.
