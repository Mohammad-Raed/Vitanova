Thermoreception1. Thermal sensation

Learning objectives

  • Identify the afferent fibres carrying thermal and nociceptive signals.
  • Explain why pain is a perception, not just a sensation.
  • Define the core terminology (allodynia, hyperalgesia, paresthesia…).
  • Describe gate control and endogenous modulation.
  • Outline six management strategies.
Stimulus→Transduction→Afferent transmission→Synapse(s)→Sensation / perception

Four attributes of any stimulus

Every stimulus is defined by modality, intensity, location and duration. Temperature and pain are somatosensory modalities whose afferent cell bodies lie in the dorsal root ganglia (the trigeminal ganglion for the face).

Types and receptors

Four thermal sensations — cold, cool, warm, hot — arise from the difference between the stimulus temperature and normal skin temperature (≈ 32 °C), not from absolute temperature.

TRP channels (temperature and chemical sensors)

Transient receptor potential channels have six transmembrane domains, with the pore between S5 and S6. All are gated by temperature and chemical ligands; a neuron's thermal sensitivity depends on which TRP channels it expresses. At resting skin temperature (32 °C) only TRPV4 and some TRPV3 are active.

Walk it through: Sip a menthol mint and the mouth feels cool even though nothing is cold: menthol opens TRPM8, the cold channel. Bite a chili and it feels hot: capsaicin opens TRPV1, the heat channel (> 43 °C). Same neurons, different trigger.

Capsaicin and TRPV1

  • Capsaicin (hot peppers) selectively activates nociceptive neurons by opening TRPV1, which admits Ca²⁺ and Na⁺ and depolarises the neuron.
  • Tissue-damage chemicals lower the temperature threshold of TRPV1, explaining heat hyperalgesia in injured skin; it is absent in TRPV1-knockout mice.
  • Birds lack TRPV1, so they are unaffected by capsaicin.
  • At high doses capsaicin is analgesic: it desensitises pain fibres and depletes substance P (patches and ointments for arthritis, shingles, psoriasis).

Nociception2. Pain and nociceptors

Definition

Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage. It is normally protective, and it can only be quantified by self-report (e.g. a 1–10 scale).

Pain is a perception, not merely a sensation: it can occur without nociceptor activation (phantom limb pain), and an identical stimulus (e.g. a needle) may or may not be experienced as painful.

Three dimensions of pain

Sensory-discriminative

Location, intensity and quality of the noxious stimulus.

Affective-motivational

Unpleasantness, urge to escape, fear and anxiety, autonomic (fight-or-flight) activation.

Cognitive-evaluative

Appraisal, cultural values and distraction; varies between individuals and cultures.

Nociceptors and fibre types

Nociceptors are free nerve endings of primary afferents that respond selectively to tissue-damaging stimuli and are not modality-specific. They project to the spinal cord (or the trigeminal nucleus for the face) via thinly myelinated Aδ or unmyelinated C fibres.

Aδ (first pain)C (second pain)
MyelinationThinNone
Diameter · velocity1–6 µm · 4–36 m/s0.2–1.5 µm · 0.4–2.0 m/s

For comparison, Aα conducts at 72–120 m/s and Aβ at 36–72 m/s. Muscle afferents are classified by diameter (groups I–IV); cutaneous afferents by conduction velocity (Aα, Aβ, Aδ, C).

Walk it through: Stub your toe. The fast Aδ fibres deliver an immediate sharp jab (first pain); seconds later the slow C fibres arrive with a dull, throbbing ache (second pain). Remember: Aδ = quick and sharp; C = slow and burning.

Nociceptor properties

  • Channels expressed: TRP channels, tetrodotoxin (TTX)-resistant Na⁺ channels, and ionotropic purinergic receptors (activated by ATP from damaged cells).
  • Mechanical nociceptors: a blunt probe produces no response despite strong pressure; a pinprick gives a brief burst; a serrated-forceps pinch gives the strongest response because it damages tissue.

Peripheral mechanisms3. Peripheral sensitisation and neurogenic inflammation

Inflammatory mediators

Damaged cells (ATP, H⁺), platelets (5-HT), macrophages (bradykinin, IL-1β, NGF, TNF-α), prostaglandins, mast cells and neutrophils (histamine), and the nerve terminal itself (substance P, CGRP).

Sequence after a burn

  1. Immediate: tissue injury → action potentials in sensory fibres (first awareness of pain) → reddening and weal.
  2. Soon after: peptides released from sensory nerves trigger histamine and other mediators → flare and hyperalgesia.
  3. Long term: secondary hyperalgesia from receptor sensitisation and altered central synaptic transmission; pain persists beyond tissue damage.

Neurogenic inflammation

  1. Bradykinin and prostaglandins activate and sensitise nociceptors.
  2. Activated terminals release substance P and CGRP.
  3. Substance P causes mast cell degranulation (histamine excites nociceptors) and plasma extravasation.
  4. CGRP dilates peripheral vessels; the resulting oedema liberates more bradykinin.
  5. The cycle spreads to adjacent healthy tissue → secondary (spreading) hyperalgesia.

Central mechanisms4. Central processing and referred pain

The classical hard-wired pathway (nociceptor → afferent → spinothalamic system) accounts only for the sensory-discriminative dimension. Threshold, intensity and even modality are determined by central processing.

Melzack, Rose & McGinty (1962)

Probes at a constant 43 °C of increasing diameter produce different percepts (spatial summation):

  • 1 mm: prickling pain
  • 5 mm: stinging pain
  • 20 mm: pleasant warmth

Not explained by the classical pathway alone

  • Referred pain
  • Trigeminal neuralgia and fibromyalgia
  • Complex regional pain syndromes
  • Phantom limb pain
  • Acupuncture analgesia

Referred pain

Pain arising from deep (visceral) structures is perceived at another body site. According to the convergence model:

  1. Visceral nociceptive afferents enter the cord at the same segment as afferents from skin, muscle and joints.
  2. Both converge on common second-order neurons that ascend in a single tract.
  3. Because cutaneous pain is far more common, the brain attributes activity in that pathway to the skin.
Walk it through: During a heart attack, cardiac afferents and left-arm skin afferents converge on the same spinal neurons. The brain, used to skin input, reads the signal as arm pain. Clinical correlate: referral zones of different organs partly overlap, notably heart and oesophagus, so cardiac pain may present as shoulder or arm pain and mimic heartburn.

Endogenous control5. Modulation of pain

Pain is perceived when inhibitory modulation is smaller than nociceptive activation. Modulation operates at several levels: spinal gate control, descending monoaminergic pathways, the endogenous opioid and endocannabinoid systems, and psychological (affective and cognitive) factors.

Gate control theory

Activity in large-diameter, non-nociceptive afferents (Aα/β from touch and stretch receptors) activates inhibitory interneurons that reduce transmission through the nociceptive pathway at the first synapse.

  1. No stimulus: a tonically active inhibitory interneuron suppresses the projection neuron → no signal.
  2. Noxious stimulus: the C fibre excites the projection neuron and switches off the interneuron → strong pain signal (gate open).
  3. Touch plus noxious stimulus: the Aβ fibre excites the inhibitory interneuron, restoring inhibition → reduced pain signal (gate partly closed).
Walk it through: Bump your elbow and you instinctively rub it: the touch input (Aβ) closes the gate on the pain input (C). Applications: rubbing a bumped elbow; acupuncture, TENS, massage. It remains a “theory” because the interneurons are poorly characterised.

Higher control and pathways

Higher control arises from the anterior cingulate gyrus and the PAG.

  • Normal pathway: the afferent releases substance P → dorsal-horn neurons → thalamus (perception), somatosensory cortex (localisation), reticular formation (arousal), hypothalamus and limbic system (emotional response).
  • Analgesic pathway: PAG, medulla and reticular formation activate a dorsal-horn inhibitory interneuron → endogenous opioid binds opiate receptors on the afferent terminal → substance P release is blocked → no pain perception. Oxycodone acts at the same receptors.

Clinical6. Chronic pain and management

Persistence

Prolonged pain alters the nervous system (central plasticity), so pain can outlast tissue damage. Peripheral sensitisation and central plasticity make it self-sustaining.

Neuropathic pain

Results from direct injury to peripheral or central nerves; typically burning or electric, out of proportion to tissue injury, and not necessarily nociceptor-driven.

  • Peripheral: prolonged neurogenic inflammation. Central: CNS lesions, e.g. after stroke.
  • Only some cases respond to opioids; gabapentin and pregabalin are used.

Six management strategies

Management is difficult because pain has sensory, affective and cognitive components, can become self-sustaining, and differs in type (nociceptive vs neuropathic).

#StrategyExamples
1Deliver anaesthetic locally, avoiding unwanted sites of actionTopical, epidural, spinal; local anaesthetics and nerve blocks; low-dose lidocaine blocks small-diameter fibres
2Target peripheral sensitisationAntihistamines, NSAIDs, ice
3Apply gate theoryAcupuncture, exercise, massage, TENS
4Activate endogenous modulationExercise (endorphins), meditation

6Prevent rather than treatPre-operative local anaesthetics; early opioids (patches, patient-controlled delivery) to avoid chronic pain
