1. Objectives and the four attributes of a stimulus

After this lecture you should be able to describe muscle spindle afferents and what they respond to; define proprioception, dermatome, intrafusal muscle fibre and gamma-motoneuron; explain how fine touch is transduced and the role of rate coding; explain rapidly vs slowly adapting afferents; describe what each touch receptor detects and how its location affects its receptive field; and locate the sensory homunculus and explain why it is distorted.

Stimulus→Sensory transduction→Sensory transmission (afferent)→Synapse(s)→Motor output (efferents)/Sensation, perception

The 4 attributes of any stimulus

  • Modality: what kind of stimulus it is (touch, pain, stretch…). Decided by which receptor type is activated.
  • Intensity: how strong it is. Signalled by the firing rate.
  • Location: where it is. Signalled by which receptors (and receptive fields) are activated.
  • Duration: how long it lasts. Signalled by the time course of firing.

Proprioception = the sense of muscle length, muscle force and joint angle, i.e. awareness of where your limbs and body parts are in space and how strongly muscles are contracting.
3. Muscle receptors: the big picture

Muscle and tendon contain specialized sensory receptors that detect changes in muscle length, tendon tension and joint angles.

  • Muscle spindle: detects stretch in the muscle. It is kept “contracted” (taut) by gamma motoneurons.
  • Golgi tendon organ: detects tension in the tendon (covered in detail in Lecture 4).
  • Joint capsule receptors: report joint angle.

The “adjusters”: gamma motoneurons

  • Gamma neurons carry instructions from the spinal cord to the muscle spindle.
  • They attach to the contractile ends of the intrafusal fibres. When activated, the ends contract.
  • That pulls the middle sensory region taut, keeping it sensitive to stretch.
  • Why it matters: when a muscle shortens, its spindle could go slack and stop detecting changes properly.

The sensory fibres of the spindle

  • Ia fibres wrap around the central region of the intrafusal fibres. They detect how much the muscle is stretched and how quickly its length is changing, are especially sensitive to sudden stretch, send information very fast to the spinal cord and are key to the stretch reflex.
  • Group II fibres also detect stretch within the spindle but mainly report the current muscle length, especially in sustained stretch. They are not sensitive to the speed of stretch. They support posture and the sense of limb position.
4. Structure of the muscle spindle

The muscle spindle is a specialized sensory organ lying within skeletal muscle, parallel to the ordinary (extrafusal) fibres. A capsule surrounds a bundle of thin intrafusal muscle fibres.

  • Intrafusal fibres are wrapped by the endings of sensory nerves.
  • Ia sensory fibres: particularly sensitive to muscle stretch, i.e. a change in length.
  • Group II sensory fibres: convey the static length of the muscle.
  • Large-diameter myelinated afferent fibres.
  • Intrafusal fibres are innervated by gamma-motoneurons (efferent, causing contraction of the intrafusal fibre ends).

Types of intrafusal fibre (Fig 35-3)

  • Dynamic nuclear bag fibre and static nuclear bag fibre.
  • Nuclear chain fibres.
  • Both Ia and II endings wrap these fibres; there is a static and a dynamic gamma motor neuron (see topic 6).
5. How the spindle transduces stretch (Fig 22-6)

The muscle spindle is the principal receptor mediating proprioception. It is excited by stretch of the muscle.

Muscle is stretched→Intrafusal fibre stretches the sensory ending→Stretch-sensitive ion channels open→Current flows in (depolarization)→Receptor potential → action potentials in Ia fibre
  • Stretch-sensitive channels in the sensory nerve terminals are linked to the cytoskeleton by the protein spectrin, so stretching the membrane pulls them open.
  • The depolarizing receptor potential in a group Ia fibre is proportional to both the velocity and the amplitude of muscle stretch. Bigger/faster stretch gives a bigger receptor potential (the graph shows larger curves for greater stretch).
  • When stretch is maintained at a fixed length, the receptor potential decays to a lower value (this is the dynamic part dropping to the steady part).
  • Single-channel recordings (patch clamp): with no pressure the channel opens only sporadically and briefly; as suction (pressure) increases the channel opens more often and stays open longer, letting more current in and producing more depolarization.
Link to Lecture 4: a bigger receptor potential leads to a higher action potential rate in the afferent (rate coding).
6. Gamma motoneurons and alpha-gamma co-activation

Gamma motoneurons regulate spindle length so the spindle stays responsive over a range of muscle lengths.

  1. Sustained stretch of the muscle: Ia fibres fire (they sense the pull).
  2. Only alpha motoneurons stimulated: the main (extrafusal) muscle shortens, the spindle goes slack, and Ia firing falls silent during the contraction. The spindle did not detect anything even though the muscle contracted.
  3. Alpha and gamma stimulated together: the intrafusal ends also contract, the spindle stays taut, stretch-sensitive channels stay open, and Ia firing is “filled in” throughout the contraction.
Increased spindle tone = increased sensitivity and output.

Two types of gamma neuron (Fig 35-3)

8. Touch: the four mechanoreceptors

Touch is sensed by mechanoreceptors in the skin that respond to skin deformation and motion. Four types convey touch (Fig 21-1, 23-1).

  • RA = rapidly adapting; SA = slowly adapting; type 1 = superficial/small field; type 2 = deep/large field.
  • The two superficial receptors (Meissner, Merkel) give finer discrimination: smallest receptive fields and most sensitive to pressure from a small probe, so the most precise localization of touch.
  • Lecturer’s note: the last two (Pacinian, Ruffini) are further from the surface and detect changes in pressure.
  • Anatomy: Meissner corpuscles border each papillary ridge; Merkel cells form dense bands around sweat-gland ducts along the ridges. RA1 and SA1 fibres branch, so each fibre innervates several nearby receptor organs; RA2 and SA2 fibres each innervate only one receptor organ.
  • All four receptors are innervated by large-diameter myelinated fibres.

How combinations feel

  • Firing of all four receptors: the sensation of contact with an object.
  • Selective activation of Merkel and Ruffini: steady pressure on the skin above the receptor.
  • The same firing patterns in Meissner and Pacinian only: tingling sensation of vibration.
9. Rapidly vs slowly adapting receptors; the Pacinian corpuscle

The structure of the receptor determines its adaptation properties.

Rapidly adapting (Meissner, Pacinian)Slowly adapting (Merkel, Ruffini)
Response to constant pressureBurst at the start (and at the end), then silentKeeps firing for as long as pressure lasts (“non-adapting”)
SignalsChanges: onset, offset and the rate at which pressure is applied or removedSustained pressure, shape and size of objects

Why Pacinian corpuscles adapt rapidly (Fig 21-10)

  • It is a rapidly adapting mechanoreceptor found in skin, joint capsules and the mesentery of the abdominal wall. It has many concentric, fluid-filled lamellae of connective tissue forming a capsule around the nerve terminal.
  • When a stimulus first presses, the capsule deforms and compresses the terminal, opening stretch-sensitive channels: response at stimulus onset (one or two APs).
  • During steady pressure the capsule changes shape and the outer lamellae are compressed, absorbing the static load; deformation no longer reaches the inner core, so the nerve goes silent.
  • When pressure is removed, the capsule resumes its shape; the tissue movement stimulates the terminal again: the “off” response.
  • Rapid movements pass through the lamellae to the nerve terminal. A 110 Hz vibration gives a receptor potential and an AP for each vibratory cycle, so Pacinian corpuscles are sensitive to vibration.
Lecturer’s note: pressing on the receptor lets ions flow in through channels; then the receptor “gets used to” the pressure, ions stop entering, and you stop feeling it. Pacinian corpuscles detect changes in pressure (e.g. vibration) very well. A steady, blunt stimulus gives fewer spikes than a changing one.
10. Rate coding: intensity, duration, timing (Fig 21-8)

Rate coding: the higher the stimulus intensity, the more action potentials per second.

  • Intensity is signalled by the firing rate of individual receptors; duration by the time course of firing.
  • Slowly adapting receptor: responds as long as pressure is applied. The total number of action potentials during the stimulus is proportional to the amount of pressure. Firing is higher at first contact than during steady pressure.
  • Rapidly adapting receptor: responds at the beginning and end of the stimulus, signalling the rate at which the probe is applied and removed; silent when pressure is maintained at a fixed amplitude. Rapid motion evokes a brief burst of high-frequency spikes; slow motion evokes a longer-lasting, low-frequency train.
  • When the probe is removed from the skin, spike activity ceases.

Where the four attributes come from

  • Modality: which receptor type fires.
  • Location: the spatial distribution of the population of activated receptors; each receptor fires only if the stimulus falls in its receptive field.
  • Intensity: firing rate. Timing: time course of firing.
11. SA1 fibres encode shape and size (Fig 23-7)

Slowly adapting type 1 (SA1) fibres encode the shape and size of objects touching the hand.

  • The area of contact determines the firing rate and the number of SA1 fibres stimulated.
  • A small, sharply pointed probe concentrates all force in a small area: a small population fires intensely.
  • A medium probe excites more receptors, but the spread of force lowers peak firing; it feels less sharp.
  • A large round probe excites many receptors across the finger at low rates; the sensation is diffuse.
  • During steady pressure the firing rate is proportional to the curvature of the probe. The highest rates come from the smallest probe; the weakest from flat or gently rounded probes.

12. Receptive fields and two-point discrimination

The receptive field of a sensory neuron is the spatial domain where stimulation excites or inhibits that neuron. For a touch neuron it is the skin region where gentle touch evokes action potentials, and it includes all the receptive endings and terminal branches of that sensory fibre.

  • If a single fibre is stimulated electrically with a microelectrode, the person feels touch localized to its receptive field. The area where the sensation arises is the perceptive field.
  • A patch of skin contains many overlapping receptive fields, so sensations shift smoothly from one neuron to the next.
  • Axon terminals of sensory neurons in the CNS are arranged somatotopically (an orderly map of the body).
  • More overlapping fields at a location means finer ability to tell where the touch is (lecturer’s note). This is measured by two-point discrimination.

Two-point discrimination (Fig 23-5)

  • The two-point threshold is the minimum distance at which two stimuli are felt as distinct.
  • About 2 mm on the fingers; up to about 10 mm on the palm; about 40 mm on the arm, thigh and back.
  • The thresholds match the diameters of receptive fields in those regions.
  • The greatest discriminative capacity is in the fingertips, lips and tongue (smallest receptive fields). The hand is far better than the back; the front of the body generally has better two-point discrimination than the back.
Clinical/research link: it is possible to stimulate an afferent nerve to simulate touch at a specific receptor site in people with a prosthesis or spinal cord injury.

14. The dorsal root ganglion cell and how sensory fibres travel

The dorsal root ganglion neuron is the primary sensory receptor cell of the somatosensory system (Fig 22-1).

  • Its cell body lies in a dorsal root ganglion next to the spinal cord.
  • Its axon has two branches: one goes to the periphery, where its specialized terminal holds the receptor for a particular form of stimulus energy; the other goes to the spinal cord or brain stem, where the afferent signals are processed.
  • Afferent fibres enter via the dorsal root (to the dorsal horn); efferent fibres leave via the ventral root; both travel together in a spinal nerve. The signal first reaches the dorsal horn.
Receptor in skin/muscle→Afferent axon→Cell body in dorsal root ganglion→Dorsal root→Dorsal horn
15. Dermatomes (Fig 22-9)
Definition: a dermatome is the area of skin (and deeper tissues) supplied mainly by sensory fibres from one spinal nerve (dorsal) root.
  • The dermatomes of the 31 pairs of dorsal root nerves are projected on to the body surface and labelled by the foramen through which each nerve enters the spinal cord.
  • Roots: 8 cervical (C), 12 thoracic (T), 5 lumbar (L), 5 sacral (S) and a single coccygeal, numbered rostrocaudally in each division of the vertebral column.
  • C1 has no dorsal root, only a ventral (motor) root.
  • Facial skin, cornea, scalp, dura and intra-oral regions are supplied by the ophthalmic (I), maxillary (II) and mandibular (III) divisions of the trigeminal nerve (cranial nerve V).
  • Clinical use: dermatome maps are an important diagnostic tool to locate injury to the spinal cord and dorsal roots. If a particular root is injured, you get numbness in its dermatome.
  • Boundaries are less distinct than maps show, because the axons of one dorsal root come from several peripheral nerves, and each peripheral nerve gives fibres to several adjacent dorsal roots (overlap).

Anterolateral system (pain, itch, temperature, visceral)

  • Carried by small-diameter myelinated and unmyelinated fibres that end in the dorsal horn.
  • The information crosses the midline within the spinal cord and ascends in the contralateral anterolateral system to brain stem and thalamus.
  • Fibres ending in the brain stem form the spinoreticular and spinomesencephalic tracts; the rest form the spinothalamic tract.
17. The cerebral cortex and the sensory homunculus
  • The postcentral gyrus lies immediately behind the central sulcus in the parietal lobe.
  • It is the primary sensory (somatosensory) cortex and is organized somatotopically: the “sensory homunculus”.
  • Homunculus is Latin for “little human”: the body parts are represented over the cortical surface.
  • Some areas are over- or under-represented. Representation depends on how many receptors a body region has (receptor density), not on its physical size. Lips, tongue and fingers occupy large areas; trunk and legs occupy small areas.
  • The right body is represented on the left cortex (and vice versa).
  • For contrast, the primary motor cortex lies in front of the central sulcus.

High-yield summary

Definitions

  • Proprioception: muscle length, muscle force, joint angle.
  • Intrafusal fibre: thin fibre inside the spindle; innervated by gamma motoneurons.
  • Dermatome: skin (and deeper tissue) supplied by one dorsal root.
  • Receptive field: area where stimulation excites or inhibits that neuron.
  • Rate coding: higher intensity gives more APs per second.

Spindle

  • In parallel with muscle fibres; detects stretch.
  • Ia: length + rate of change; big, fast; stretch reflex.
  • II: static length; posture.
  • Gamma: keeps spindle taut; alpha-gamma co-activation prevents slack.
  • Static gamma: length. Dynamic gamma: speed.

Transduction

  • Stretch-sensitive channels linked to cytoskeleton (spectrin).
  • More stretch/pressure: channels open more often and longer: more depolarization.
  • Receptor potential proportional to velocity and amplitude of stretch.
  • Fixed-length stretch: receptor potential decays to a lower value.

Touch receptors

  • Meissner RA1: superficial, small field, flutter/slip.
  • Merkel SA1: superficial, small field, pressure/texture/shape.
  • Pacinian RA2: deep, large field, vibration.
  • Ruffini SA2: deep, large field, skin stretch.

Fibres

  • Ia and Ib 12–20 µm, II 6–12 µm, III 2–6 µm, IV 0.5–2 µm unmyelinated.
  • Larger diameter: faster (A-alpha 72–120 m/s; A-beta 36–72; A-delta 4–36; C 0.4–2).
  • Fine touch and proprioception: A-alpha and A-beta.

Cortex and spinal anatomy

  • Cell body in dorsal root ganglion; enters dorsal horn.
  • 31 spinal nerve pairs: 8 C, 12 T, 5 L, 5 S, 1 Co; C1 has no dorsal root.
  • Homunculus in postcentral gyrus; distortion = receptor density; right body on left cortex.

Lecture 4 · Somatic reflexes and somatosensory integration

Full breakdown

Spinal reflexes and sensorimotor integration, plus receptor potentials.

1. Objectives and the sensory-to-motor chain

After this session you should be able to: describe how a stimulus is transduced; explain what a receptor potential is and its characteristics; describe the difference between an action potential in an afferent fibre vs an interneuron or efferent nerve; describe the reflex arc and function of proprioceptive reflexes from muscle spindle afferents and Golgi tendon organs; describe a polysynaptic spinal reflex and the reflex arc and function of the flexion withdrawal and crossed extension reflexes; and describe how the gain of reflexes can be controlled.

Stimulus→Sensory transduction→Sensory transmission (afferent)→Synapse(s)→Motor output (efferents)=Reflex

The same chain leads to sensation and perception when the signal also ascends to the brain. A reflex is the version that ends in a motor output.

2. Sensory transduction and the receptor potential

A stimulus alters the receptor’s permeability, producing a graded receptor potential.

  • Stimulation usually opens channels, giving an inward flux of Na⁺ that depolarizes the receptor membrane.
  • Receptor potentials have no refractory period, so summation of rapidly successive stimuli is possible.

Coding of stimulus strength (Fig 6-3)

Stronger stimulus→Greater permeability change→Larger receptor potential→Higher rate of APs in the afferent→More neurotransmitter at afferent terminals
  • In the figure, a second, larger stimulus gives a larger receptor potential, more frequent spikes in the afferent fibre and more transmitter release at the terminals.
  • The receptor potential stays up during the stimulus and then returns to rest when the stimulus goes off; the AP train follows its size.
  • Figure-focus question: an acid-monitoring neuron would fire more if body fluid became too acidic (stronger stimulus), and less if too alkaline, assuming it is excited by acid.
3. Receptor potential to action potential (Fig 6-1)

(a) Receptor is a specialized afferent ending (e.g. Meissner, Pacinian)

  1. Stimulus opens stimulus-sensitive channels (non-specific cation); net Na⁺ entry gives the receptor potential.
  2. Local current flow between the depolarized ending and the adjacent region opens voltage-gated Na⁺ channels.
  3. Na⁺ entry initiates an action potential in the afferent fibre, which self-propagates to the CNS.

(b) Receptor is a separate cell (e.g. Merkel disc)

  1. Stimulus opens stimulus-sensitive channels; Na⁺ entry gives a receptor potential in the receptor cell.
  2. The local depolarization opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels.
  3. Ca²⁺ entry triggers exocytosis of neurotransmitter.
  4. Neurotransmitter binds chemically gated receptor-channels on the afferent ending, letting Na⁺ in.
  5. The resulting depolarization opens voltage-gated Na⁺ channels in the adjacent region.
  6. Na⁺ entry initiates an AP in the afferent fibre that propagates to the CNS.
Difference: in (a) the receptor potential is produced in the afferent ending itself; in (b) a separate receptor cell must release transmitter onto the afferent first.

5. Spinal cord anatomy review (Fig 5-24)
  • White matter: axons of descending or ascending neurons.
  • Gray matter: neurons and glia living within the cord.
  • Dorsal roots: afferent (sensory) axons entering the cord. The cell bodies of afferent neurons are in the dorsal root ganglion.
  • Ventral roots: efferent (motor) axons exiting the cord. Cell bodies of efferent neurons are in the gray matter.
  • Dorsal horn: sensory processing. Ventral horn: motor output.
  • Afferent and efferent fibres are enclosed together within a spinal nerve.
  • Interneurons in the gray matter link afferents to efferents.
The spinal cord is not just a highway carrying messages up and down to and from the brain; it does much more, including reflex integration.
6. Somatic reflexes and the reflex arc

Somatic reflexes are automatic responses brought about by skeletal muscle contraction that take place without conscious effort. Example: protective reflexes to withdraw from painful stimuli.

Components of a reflex arc (Fig 8.26)

Receptor→Afferent pathway→Integrating centre (spinal cord)→Efferent pathway→Effector (muscle)
  • Monosynaptic reflex: one synapse between afferent and motoneuron.
  • Disynaptic / polysynaptic reflex: one or more interneurons are inserted.

  • Spindle = intrafusal fibres wrapped by sensory endings; Ia sensitive to change in length; group II convey static length; large-diameter myelinated afferents; intrafusal fibres innervated by gamma motoneurons.
8. The monosynaptic stretch reflex (knee jerk)

Other names: stretch reflex, tendon tap reflex, knee-jerk reflex, patellar tendon reflex, monosynaptic reflex, group Ia reflex.

Tap on patellar tendon→Quadriceps stretched→Muscle spindles stretched (Ia afferent fires)→Ia synapses directly on alpha motoneuron in cord→Quadriceps (extensor) contracts
  1. Muscle spindles detect stretch and send APs to the CNS (afferent).
  2. The Ia afferent excites alpha motoneurons of the stretched muscle and its synergists (efferent).
  3. The Ia afferent also activates an inhibitory interneuron that inhibits the antagonists (this part is disynaptic/polysynaptic).

The load-on-the-arm example (Fig 13.3)

  1. A load is added to the muscle.
  2. Muscle and spindle stretch as the arm extends; spindle afferents fire more frequently.
  3. Reflex contraction through alpha motoneurons restores arm position and prevents damage from over-stretching. As the muscle contracts, afferent firing decreases: negative feedback.
Figure question answer: the neuron that fires to contract the extrafusal fibres is the muscle alpha motor neuron (option a).

Points about the stretch reflex

  • Almost all muscles have a stretch reflex; the patellar tendon/quadriceps is just convenient to test.
  • It is graded: a harder tap gives a larger response (up to a point).
  • It occurs in addition to other actions of the large-diameter fibres, e.g. travelling up the cord to higher centres (axon collaterals are abundant in the nervous system).
  • It can be elicited by electrical stimulation of the peripheral nerve: then called the H reflex (after Hoffmann).
  • In daily life it is elicited by moving the limb.

Uses of the stretch reflex

  • Rapid correction of unexpected muscle stretch (can prevent you from falling).
  • Sensory feedback from muscles helps activate them during movement.
9. Controlling the stretch reflex: spindle (gamma), synapse, motoneuron
Sensory afferent (muscle spindle)→Synapse in spinal cord→Motoneuron (efferent)

The monosynaptic arc has three sites where the “gain” of the reflex can be changed.

1. The sensory afferent (muscle spindle)

  • Spindle excitability is controlled by the nervous system through gamma motoneurons to the intrafusal fibres.
  • Tension in the intrafusal fibres sets spindle sensitivity. More gamma activity gives higher spindle tone and sensitivity, so more transduction and output.
  • If the extrafusal muscle shortens with alpha activity alone, the spindle goes slack and Ia fibres become unresponsive; co-activating alpha and gamma keeps the spindle responsive (Ia response “filled in”).

2. The synapse: presynaptic inhibition

  • Controls the strength of the connection between the afferent and the motoneuron.
  • Due to GABAergic axo-axonal synapses on the afferent terminal, which decrease the transmitter released from the presynaptic terminal.

3. The motoneuron

  • If motoneurons are more excitable, the same stretch gives a larger contraction (new motoneurons are recruited, others fire more APs).
  • Example: the Jendrassik manoeuvre gives background excitation to leg motoneurons and increases the stretch reflex.
10. Postsynaptic potentials and presynaptic inhibition (Figs 4-16, 4-17)

Revision of postsynaptic potentials (Fig 4-16)

SituationWhat happens
(a) No summationA second EPSP arriving after the first has died off has the same size; threshold is not reached.
(b) Temporal summationA second EPSP before the first dies off adds onto it; may bring the cell to threshold.
(c) Spatial summationSimultaneous EPSPs from two or more excitatory inputs (Ex1 + Ex2) sum and may reach threshold.
(d) EPSP–IPSP cancellationSimultaneous excitatory (Ex1) and inhibitory (In1) input cancel; no change in potential.

In reality many EPSPs must sum to reach threshold; the figure simplifies to two. A neuron simulator link (Neuronify) is given for practice.

Presynaptic inhibition (Fig 4-17)

  • Excitatory terminal A ends on cell C and is itself innervated by inhibitory terminal B.
  • Stimulating A alone gives an EPSP in C. Stimulating A and B together prevents release from A, so no EPSP appears in C.
  • It selectively depresses input from A without suppressing other excitatory inputs: stimulating D still gives an EPSP even with B active, because B only inhibits A.
  • Figure-focus question (D innervated by excitatory terminal E): by the same logic, stimulating E and D together would increase release from D and give a larger EPSP in C (presynaptic facilitation).
11. Jendrassik manoeuvre

A way to enhance reflex testing by adding background excitation to motoneurons, so the reflex is larger and easier to see.

Reflex being testedReinforcing action (from the slide)
Lower extremity (e.g. patellar)Interlock the fingers and pull the elbows apart
Upper extremity (e.g. biceps)Cross the ankles and attempt to abduct (pull apart) the legs

The effort in a distant muscle group raises the excitability of the motoneurons for the tested muscle, which is why the response grows (control site number 3).

12. Golgi tendon organs and Ib reflexes

Golgi tendon organs are tension receptors in series with the muscle fibres; their afferents are group Ib (slightly smaller than Ia spindle afferents). They are activated by muscle force, e.g. during contraction.

Receptor mechanism (Fig 35-6a)

When the tendon organ is stretched (usually by muscle contraction), the Ib afferent axon is compressed by collagen fibres and its firing rate increases.

Classical reflex action (at rest)

  • Activation at rest leads to inhibition of motoneurons innervating the same muscle (roughly the opposite of the stretch reflex), through Ib inhibitory interneurons.
  • In the figure Ib input inhibits the same muscle and synergists, and excites the antagonist.
  • It was therefore thought to protect muscle from damage by excessive force.
The “protection” idea is outdated. Dr McCrea and colleagues (University of Manitoba) showed excitatory Golgi tendon reflexes during locomotion, which reinforce motor activity and increase muscle force production.

State-dependent reflex reversal (Fig 35-7)

StateInterneuronsEffect on ankle extensor motoneuron

WalkingIb inhibitory interneurons inhibited; excitatory interneurons facilitated by the command system for walkingDepolarization (about 4 mV in the record): an excitatory Ib pathway opens

Why the Golgi tendon reflex is complicated

Ib inhibitory interneurons receive input not only from tendon organs but also from muscle spindles, joint and cutaneous receptors and descending pathways (convergence).

Proprioceptive reflexes during movement

  • Stretch reflexes allow rapid correction of unanticipated changes in muscle length (this can keep you from falling).
  • Both Golgi tendon organ and spindle afferents give ongoing feedback, reinforce the motor pattern and can alter it if appropriate (e.g. stumbling).
13. Flexion withdrawal reflex

Also called the flexion reflex or withdrawal reflex. Example: touching a hot pot (Fig 8.26).

  1. A painful heat stimulus activates thermal pain receptors in the finger.
  2. APs are generated in the afferent pathway and travel to the spinal cord through the dorsal root.
  3. The spinal cord is the integrating centre, stimulating:
    • Excitatory interneurons that excite efferent motoneurons to the biceps (flexor).
    • Inhibitory interneurons that inhibit efferent motoneurons to the triceps (extensor).
    • Interneurons that are part of the ascending pathway to the brain.
  4. One efferent pathway makes the biceps contract; the other relaxes the triceps, preventing counterproductive contraction of the antagonist.
  5. The biceps are the effectors; flexion of the elbow pulls the hand away, completing the reflex arc.
  • Arc: nociceptive afferent → interneuron(s) → motoneurons; at least disynaptic, maybe polysynaptic.
  • Result: flexor motoneurons of the whole limb activated, extensor motoneurons inhibited, extremity rapidly moved away (protective).
  • It runs in addition to the ascending pathways that give pain perception, but the reflex occurs before the perception of pain.
14. Crossed extension reflex

Question on the slide: what would happen if you had a flexion withdrawal reflex in your leg? If the injured leg lifted and nothing supported the body on the other side, you would fall. The crossed extension reflex prevents this.

Pain receptor in foot (e.g. nail)→Nociceptive afferent→Interneuron(s) in cord→Ipsilateral: flexors on, extensors off/Contralateral: extensors on, flexors off
  • Activated simultaneously whenever a flexion withdrawal is started in a leg.
  • Crossed means the effect is on the contralateral limb (opposite the stimulus).
  • Arc: nociceptive afferents → interneuron(s) → contralateral extensor motoneurons. Like the flexion reflex it is at least disynaptic, probably longer.
  • Results: activation of extensor motoneurons and inhibition of flexor motoneurons of the contralateral limb, causing extension and weight support.
  • This lets the other limb perform a flexion withdrawal. It keeps you from falling when you step on something sharp.

15. Controlling di- and polysynaptic reflexes
  1. The afferent: maybe, but not like muscle spindles (which have gamma control).
  2. The synapse(s): e.g. presynaptic inhibition.
  3. The interneuron(s): if the interneuron is inhibited it is harder to activate, so less transmission through the reflex pathway.
  4. The motoneuron: changing its excitability changes the size of the reflex.
Key difference: interneurons give an additional site of control compared with the monosynaptic reflex.
Lecture 4 · Exam notes

High-yield summary

Transduction

  • Stimulus: permeability change: graded receptor potential (Na⁺ in).
  • No refractory period: summation.
  • Stronger stimulus: bigger RP: higher AP rate.
  • Separate receptor cell (Merkel): Ca²⁺ entry, transmitter release, then AP.

AP initiation site

  • Afferent: peripheral end next to receptor.
  • Interneuron and efferent: axon hillock.

Spinal cord

  • Dorsal root: afferent in. Ventral root: efferent out.
  • Dorsal horn: sensory. Ventral horn: motor.
  • White matter: axons. Gray matter: neurons and glia.

Stretch reflex (Ia)

  • Monosynaptic: spindle Ia → alpha motoneuron of same muscle and synergists.
  • Antagonist inhibited via Ia inhibitory interneuron.
  • Negative feedback; graded; H reflex = electrical.
  • Uses: correct sudden stretch; assist movement.

Controlling gain

  • Afferent: gamma motoneurons (spindle tone).
  • Synapse: presynaptic inhibition (GABA axo-axonal).
  • Motoneuron excitability (Jendrassik).
  • Polysynaptic: also interneurons.

Golgi tendon (Ib)

  • Tension receptor in series; collagen compresses Ib axon.
  • At rest: inhibits same muscle (Ib interneuron).
  • Locomotion: reversed to excitation (McCrea).
  • Protection idea is outdated.
