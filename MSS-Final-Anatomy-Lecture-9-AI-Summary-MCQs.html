Lecture 9 · Part 1 — Posterior compartment

A transverse intermuscular septum splits it into a superficial and a deep group. Supply: posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve (all muscles).

Superficial group: triceps surae

Triceps surae = soleus + two heads of gastrocnemius. They share one insertion on the posterior calcaneus through the calcaneal (Achilles) tendon, the strongest tendon in the body. They give the main propulsive force in walking and running.

Unlocking the knee: with the foot planted, popliteus rotates the femur laterally on the fixed tibia. With the foot free, it rotates the tibia medially on the femur. Both descriptions refer to the same relative motion.
Popliteus and the menisci: its tendon lies between the lateral collateral ligament and the lateral meniscus, so the lateral meniscus stays mobile and is injured less often. The deep medial collateral ligament attaches to the medial meniscus, limiting its mobility.

Posterior tibial artery and structures behind the medial malleolus

The posterior tibial artery is one of the terminal pathways from the popliteal artery. It runs deep to gastrocnemius and soleus, passes behind the medial malleolus under the flexor retinaculum and ends as the medial and lateral plantar arteries. In the lower leg it is covered only by skin and fascia, which is where its pulse is felt.

Tom Does a Very Nice Hat (medial → lateral): Tibialis posterior, flexor Digitorum longus, posterior tibial Vessels, tibial Nerve, flexor Hallucis longus. The flexor retinaculum forms the roof of the tarsal tunnel. These structures are listed from anterior to posterior as they curve behind the medial malleolus.

Small saphenous vein

Arises from the lateral dorsal venous arch, ascends behind the lateral malleolus with the sural nerve, follows the lateral edge of the Achilles tendon, runs up the middle of the calf, pierces the deep fascia and passes between the two heads of gastrocnemius. It ends in the popliteal vein (or great saphenous vein, or splits to join both) and communicates with the deep veins. It has many valves.

Lecture 9 · Part 2 — Popliteal fossa

Diamond-shaped intermuscular space behind the knee, most obvious when the knee is flexed.

  • Superomedial: semitendinosus + semimembranosus
  • Superolateral: biceps femoris
  • Inferomedial: medial head of gastrocnemius
  • Inferolateral: lateral head of gastrocnemius + plantaris
  • Roof: skin, deep fascia, small saphenous vein (plus posterior cutaneous nerve of thigh)
  • Floor: popliteal surface of femur, knee capsule (oblique popliteal ligament), popliteus, upper tibia
  • Contents: popliteal artery and vein, small saphenous vein (end), tibial and common fibular nerves, posterior cutaneous nerve of thigh, genicular branch of obturator nerve, fat and lymph nodes
Superficial → deep in the midline: tibial nerve → popliteal vein → popliteal artery. The artery is the deepest, which is why the popliteal pulse is hard to feel (best with the knee flexed).
StructureKey points
Popliteal arteryContinuation of the femoral artery, entering through the adductor hiatus. It appears at the upper medial side under semimembranosus. Near the lower border of popliteus it gives rise to the anterior and posterior tibial pathways. In vascular anatomy, the short segment between the anterior tibial takeoff and the split into posterior tibial and fibular arteries is called the tibioperoneal trunk. Gives muscular and articular (genicular) branches.
Popliteal veinFormed at the lower border of popliteus by the venae comitantes of the anterior and posterior tibial arteries. Ascends, crosses behind the artery to lie on its lateral side, and becomes the femoral vein at the adductor hiatus. Tributaries: veins matching the artery's branches and the small saphenous vein.
Genicular anastomosisBranches of the femoral, popliteal and anterior/posterior tibial arteries. Maintains blood flow when the popliteal artery narrows in extreme knee flexion.
Common fibular nerveRuns along the biceps femoris tendon and winds round the neck of the fibula. It is subcutaneous there and can be rolled against the bone, so it is the most commonly injured nerve of the leg.

Common fibular nerve injury → foot drop
  • Cause: direct trauma or fracture of the upper fibula (neck).
  • Loss of the anterior compartment (dorsiflexion, toe extension, deep fibular) and lateral compartment (eversion, superficial fibular).
  • Result: the foot hangs plantar flexed and inverted, giving a high-stepping gait; sensory loss over the lateral leg and dorsum of the foot.
  • Posterior compartment (tibial nerve) still works, so plantar flexion is intact.
Ruptured calcaneal (Achilles) tendon
  • Common in middle-aged men; typical in tennis players and sprinters.
  • Happens at the narrowest part, about 5 cm above the insertion.
  • Push-off and single-leg heel rise are impaired. Other muscles can still produce some active plantar flexion.
  • Calf-squeeze (Thompson) test: squeezing the calf normally pulls on the intact Achilles tendon and plantar flexes the foot. Little or no movement supports a rupture.

Gastrocnemius / soleus tears: severe localised pain and swelling over the damaged muscle.

Acute compartment syndrome: swelling or bleeding within a closed fascial space raises pressure and threatens tissue perfusion. Disproportionate pain and pain on passive stretch are important clues, even when distal pulses are present. Passive plantar flexion stretches the anterior compartment.
Baker cyst: the classic posteromedial location is between semimembranosus and the medial head of gastrocnemius. On a spotter, name both tendons before identifying the cyst.

Movements

Ankle: dorsiflexion and plantar flexion. Intertarsal (subtalar) joints: inversion and eversion. Toes: flexion and extension. Inverters: tibialis anterior and posterior. Evertors: fibularis longus, brevis, tertius.

Quick recall

Say the answer aloud, then open the card to check it.

Trace arterial flow from the thigh to the dorsum.

Femoral artery → adductor hiatus → popliteal artery → anterior tibial artery → dorsalis pedis.

Where is the tibial nerve in the tarsal tunnel?

Between the posterior tibial vessels and FHL in the anterior-to-posterior sequence.

What crosses the fossa close to the biceps femoris tendon?

The common fibular nerve. Follow it toward the fibular neck to distinguish it from the tibial nerve in the center of the fossa.

Why test plantar flexion with the knee bent?

Knee flexion shortens gastrocnemius and reduces its contribution. Soleus crosses only the ankle and still produces plantar flexion.
