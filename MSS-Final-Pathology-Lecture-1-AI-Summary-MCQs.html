Describe the skin lesion

Start with what you can see and feel: is it flat, raised and solid, or fluid-filled? Then describe the surface. The 5 mm (millimetre) cutoff below follows this lecture; other references may use different cutoffs.

PatternTerms and clues
Flat colour changeMacule ≤5 mm; patch >5 mm. Neither is palpable.
Raised, solidPapule ≤5 mm; nodule >5 mm and often deeper. A plaque is broad and flat-topped, sometimes formed by merging papules.
Raised, fluid or pusVesicle ≤5 mm; bulla >5 mm. A pustule contains pus. A wheal is a transient raised area caused by dermal oedema, as in hives.
Surface changesExcoriation: scratch damage that breaks the epidermis. Lichenification: thick skin with accentuated markings from repeated rubbing. Scale: flakes from the stratum corneum.

Histology terms

Orient first: The stratum corneum is normally anucleate. Rete ridges extend down from the epidermis; dermal papillae project up from the dermis. Epidermal layers from deep to superficial: basale → spinosum → granulosum → lucidum (thick skin only) → corneum.

TermWhat to look for
AcanthosisThickening of the viable epidermis. Regularly elongated rete ridges are typical of psoriasis; acanthosis also occurs in chronic eczema.
HyperkeratosisThick stratum corneum. It can contribute to visible scale.
ParakeratosisRetained nuclei in the cornified layer. This is abnormal in most skin but can be physiological in some oral mucosa.
SpongiosisOedema between keratinocytes. The spaces widen and intercellular bridges become visible; severe oedema can form an intraepidermal vesicle.
PapillomatosisRaised or undulating surface over enlarged dermal papillae.
DyskeratosisPremature keratinisation of individual cells below the granular layer. It can accompany dysplasia.
Epidermal oedema

Eczema

Spongiotic dermatitis describes a biopsy pattern. Several types of eczema share it, so exposure history and distribution help identify the subtype.

Acute: oedema and vesicles

Itchy red papules may develop vesicles, ooze and crust. Histology shows spongiosis, papillary dermal oedema and superficial perivascular inflammation. Eosinophils may be prominent in drug-related disease.

Chronic: thickening and scale

Repeated rubbing and inflammation give thick, scaly, lichenified plaques. Biopsy shows acanthosis and hyperkeratosis with less spongiosis.

Five clinical subtypes

  • Allergic contact: A sensitiser binds skin proteins. Antigen-presenting cells such as Langerhans cells activate T cells in draining lymph nodes. On re-exposure, memory T cells drive a delayed type IV reaction, usually at the contact site.
  • Atopic: A defective barrier and type 2 inflammation reinforce one another. T helper 2 cells produce cytokines including interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, IL-13 and IL-31. Immunoglobulin E (IgE) is often elevated. The atopic triad is eczema, asthma and allergic rhinitis.
  • Irritant contact: Direct injury from a chemical or physical exposure; prior immune sensitisation is unnecessary.
  • Drug-related: A medication can trigger an eczematous reaction; eosinophils support the clue but are not specific.
  • Photoeczematous: An abnormal response to light, typically on exposed skin.

Atopic distribution changes with age

Infants commonly have cheek, scalp and extensor involvement. In children the flexures become more prominent; hands, feet or flexures may be affected later. Some cases improve with age, while others persist.

Keratinocyte hyperproliferation

Psoriasis

T-cell cytokines drive keratinocyte proliferation in genetically susceptible people. The classic lesion is a sharply demarcated pink or salmon plaque with silvery scale, especially on the elbows, knees, scalp and lower back.

  • Auspitz sign: Pinpoint bleeding after scale is lifted. Thin suprapapillary epidermis overlies dilated papillary vessels.
  • Koebner phenomenon: New lesions appear where the skin was injured.
  • Nails: Pitting, thickening, crumbling and onycholysis (nail-plate separation). The lecture quotes about 30%; estimates vary.

Read the biopsy from top to bottom

  • Corneum: Hyperkeratosis and parakeratosis; neutrophils here form Munro microabscesses.
  • Viable epidermis: Reduced granular layer, regular acanthosis and thin suprapapillary plates. Neutrophils in spongiotic epidermis form pustules of Kogoj.
  • Dermis: Dilated, tortuous papillary capillaries and superficial inflammation. Fungal infection can mimic parts of this pattern; fungal stains can help when suspected.

Associations: Psoriatic arthritis and increased cardiovascular risk. The lecture quotes up to 10% for arthritis; prevalence estimates vary across studies.

Treatment overview: Topical agents for limited disease; phototherapy or systemic treatments such as methotrexate or tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors for selected patients with more extensive disease.

Pilosebaceous unit

Acne vulgaris

Acne affects the hair follicle and its sebaceous gland, especially on the face, chest and back.

  1. Plug: Keratin accumulates at the follicular opening.
  2. Sebum: Androgens increase sebaceous activity.
  3. Microbe: Cutibacterium acnes (formerly Propionibacterium acnes) contributes to inflammation.
  4. Inflammation: The follicle can rupture and release keratin and lipid into the dermis.

Open comedones are blackheads; closed comedones are whiteheads. Papules, pustules and deep nodules are inflammatory lesions that can scar.

What changes after rupture?

The dilated follicular opening contains keratin and lipid. Once the wall ruptures, neutrophils arrive early; a foreign-body granulomatous response can follow.

Treatment link: Antibiotics can be part of treatment for inflammatory acne. Isotretinoin reduces sebaceous activity and is used for severe acne; it is teratogenic.

Subcutaneous fat

Panniculitis

Inflammation sits below the dermis, in subcutaneous fat. Tender, poorly defined red nodules on the shins are a common presentation, especially in erythema nodosum. Fever or malaise can accompany them.

Find the centre of inflammation

  • Septal: Inflammation follows fibrous partitions between fat lobules. Erythema nodosum is the classic example, usually without vasculitis.
  • Lobular: Inflammation centres within fat lobules. Examples include pancreatic, lupus and some physical or factitial panniculitides. Either pattern may occur with or without vasculitis.
  • Biopsy trap: The epidermis and dermis can look normal. The sample must contain enough subcutaneous fat to assess the pattern and any fat necrosis.
