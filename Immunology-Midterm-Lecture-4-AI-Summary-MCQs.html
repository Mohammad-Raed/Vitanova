Part 1

Explanation

Phagocytosis: five steps

Performed by neutrophils, monocytes (blood) and macrophages (tissue).

1 · Chemotaxis→2 · Recognition and attachment→3 · Engulfment (phagosome)→4 · Phagolysosome and killing→5 · Debris expelled / antigen presented
  • Movement: cells move up a gradient of C5a, CXCL8, bacterial fMLP peptides and LTB4.
  • Attachment is greatly enhanced by opsonins: C3b and IgG (also CRP and MBL).
  • Engulfment: pseudopods surround the microbe → phagosome (a vesicle containing the microbe).
  • Killing: fusion with lysosomes and granules.
  • Debris is expelled; peptides can be presented to T cells.
  • Encapsulated bacteria need opsonisation, so asplenia raises infection risk.
Flag: the slide lists the steps in prose and graphics, so the five step names above are a standard grouping of the slide bullets. The note-taker adds a red box saying that for capsulated bacteria “steps 4 and 5 fail”. That conflicts with the slide, where the capsule mainly interferes with opsonisation, attachment and engulfment. Trust the slide wording.

Encapsulated bacteria and the spleen

  • Capsules (mostly polysaccharide) act as an antiphagocytic virulence factor.
  • Examples: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type B, Neisseria meningitidis, E. coli, Salmonella, Klebsiella pneumoniae, group B Strep.
  • They are opsonised (by complement), then cleared by the spleen, which is the blood filter and contains the macrophages that phagocytose them.
  • Asplenic patients lose this ability, face a risk of severe infections, and need vaccines against N. meningitidis, S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae.
Flag: the capsule slide is an added extra, not lecturer content, and it states that Bacillus anthracis (polypeptide capsule) and Streptococcus pyogenes (hyaluronic acid capsule) are exceptions to the “polysaccharide capsule” rule. Extra

Leukocyte recruitment to tissues

Rolling (E- and P-selectins on activated endothelium)→Chemokines switch integrins to high affinity→Firm adhesion (LFA-1, Mac-1 bind ICAM-1)→Transmigration between endothelial cells
  • Timing: neutrophils arrive first (6–24 h), then monocytes (24–48 h).
  • Clinical links: LAD-I (CD18 defect: delayed cord separation, infections without pus, neutrophilia); natalizumab (anti-α4 integrin) treats multiple sclerosis.

Extra The lecturer added nothing beyond the slide here. The note-taker explains that vessels dilate near the invasion, leukocytes already in the blood move to the endothelium, and the steps above follow.

Acute inflammation

  • Purpose: deliver cells and plasma proteins, contain the microbe, begin repair.
  • ↑ blood flow → redness, heat; endothelial retraction → exudate → swelling, pain.
  • Mediators: histamine (immediate), prostaglandins, leukotrienes, bradykinin, IL-1, TNF.
  • Cardinal signs: rubor, calor, tumor, dolor plus loss of function.

Fever

LPS and microbial products→IL-1, IL-6, TNF from macrophages→COX-2 → PGE₂ in the hypothalamus raises the set point→Vasoconstriction, shivering, chills
  • Benefits: slows many pathogens; boosts phagocytes, lymphocytes and interferons.
  • Risks: tachycardia, dehydration, febrile seizures in young children.
  • NSAIDs and paracetamol block PGE₂; they treat discomfort, not the number.
  • Heat stroke does not reset the set point, so antipyretics fail.

Acute-phase response

  • Occurs after infection, trauma, surgery, burns, MI or cancer (it can look similar after pneumonia or a hip replacement).
  • IL-6 (with IL-1 and TNF) acts on hepatocytes.
  • Rise: CRP, serum amyloid A, fibrinogen, haptoglobin, ferritin, hepcidin, α1-antitrypsin, complement. Fall: albumin and transferrin.

CRP, procalcitonin and ESR

  • CRP is a pentraxin made by the liver (driven by IL-6). It binds phosphocholine on microbes and damaged cells, opsonises, recruits C1q and activates the classical complement pathway.
  • CRP kinetics: rises within 6–8 h, peaks at about 48 h, can rise up to 1000×; half-life about 19 h. The lecturer emphasised these timings (per the note-taker).
  • hs-CRP detects low CRP levels and is mainly used for cardiovascular risk: <1 / 1–3 / >3 mg/L = low / average / high risk.
  • Procalcitonin: rises in 3–6 h, mainly in systemic bacterial infection, and guides starting and stopping antibiotics.
  • ESR: slow and indirect (via fibrinogen); affected by anaemia, age and pregnancy.
  • Inflammation is now a treatment target: low-dose colchicine approved (2023) for heart disease.
Order of peak after bacterial infection: procalcitonin first (about 24 h), then CRP (about 48 h), and ESR last (about 5 days).
Flag: the slide says procalcitonin “rises in 3–6 h” but the added extra says it “peaks first (24 h)”. These are not contradictory: one is when it starts rising, the other is when it peaks. The note-taker also says a high CRP indicates infection or injury and also links to clots and heart disease.

Complement (overview only)

The full complement lecture comes later; this slide is a preview to study as written.

  • Mostly made in the liver; circulates inactive; each activated enzyme cleaves the next and amplifies.
  • Three pathways: classical (antibody, CRP), lectin (MBL), alternative (microbial surfaces).
  • Functions: lysis (MAC), opsonisation (C3b), inflammation and chemotaxis (C3a, C5a), immune-complex clearance.
  • Regulators: C1-inhibitor, DAF (CD55), CD59.
  • Deficiency links: C5–C9 → recurrent Neisseria; C1-INH → hereditary angioedema; CD55/59 loss → PNH.
  • Drugs: eculizumab/ravulizumab (C5), pegcetacoplan (C3), iptacopan (oral factor B); vaccinate against meningococcus.

Part 2

High-yield summary

  • Phagocytosis: chemotaxis (C5a, CXCL8, fMLP, LTB4) → attachment (opsonins C3b, IgG, CRP, MBL) → phagosome → fusion with lysosomes → debris out / antigen presented.
  • Asplenia → poor clearance of encapsulated bacteria (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae b, N. meningitidis); vaccinate.
  • Recruitment: selectins (rolling) → chemokine-activated integrins (LFA-1, Mac-1) bind ICAM-1 → transmigration. Neutrophils 6–24 h, monocytes 24–48 h.
  • LAD-I: delayed cord separation, infections without pus, neutrophilia. Natalizumab: anti-α4 integrin for MS.
  • Killing: NADPH oxidase → superoxide → H₂O₂; MPO → HOCl; iNOS → NO; NETs. CGD: catalase-positive infections + granulomas; DHR flow cytometry; prophylaxis, IFN-γ, stem-cell transplant.
  • Inflammation: rubor, calor, tumor, dolor + loss of function. Acute = neutrophils; chronic = macrophages/lymphocytes.
  • Fever: IL-1/IL-6/TNF → COX-2 → PGE₂. NSAIDs/paracetamol block PGE₂; heat stroke does not respond.
  • Acute-phase: up = CRP, SAA, fibrinogen, haptoglobin, ferritin, hepcidin, α1-antitrypsin, complement; down = albumin, transferrin.
  • Kinetics: procalcitonin (3–6 h rise, bacterial) → CRP (6–8 h, peak ≈ 48 h, t½ ≈ 19 h) → ESR (slowest, ≈ 5 d).
  • Complement: classical/lectin/alternative → MAC, C3b opsonisation, C3a/C5a inflammation. C5–C9 deficiency → Neisseria; C1-INH → hereditary angioedema; CD55/59 → PNH; eculizumab for C5.
