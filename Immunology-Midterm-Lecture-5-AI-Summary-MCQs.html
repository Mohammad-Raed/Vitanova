Cytokines, innate cells and the start of adaptive immunity

Part 1

Explanation

Cytokines: messengers of natural immunity

Cytokines are small secreted proteins that carry messages between cells, locally or systemically. Families: interleukins (IL), interferons (IFN), chemokines, tumour necrosis factor (TNF). The note-taker stresses that a cytokine only passes information; the target cell is free to respond or not. Extra

CytokineKey action (memorise)
TNF and IL-1Activate endothelium; cause fever
IL-6Fever and acute-phase proteins (CRP, procalcitonin)
CXCL8 (IL-8)Recruits neutrophils
IL-12Activates NK cells → IFN-γ → macrophage activation
Type I IFN (α, β)Antiviral state; activates NK cells
  • Too much = cytokine storm: sepsis, CAR-T cytokine release syndrome, severe COVID-19.
  • Blocking drugs: anti-TNF (screen for latent TB first), anakinra (IL-1), tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor).
Why TB screening? Blocking TNF can reactivate latent TB, so patients are screened first. (The same link is made in the note-taker’s MCQ answer key.)

Phagocytes: neutrophils and macrophages

Neutrophil (PMN)

  • 40–75% of blood leukocytes; multilobed nucleus; granules full of killing enzymes.
  • First to arrive; die within hours–days → pus (dead neutrophils).
  • Best against bacteria and fungi.
  • Neutropenia: <1.5 × 10⁹/L; <0.5 = severe, with high infection risk.

Monocyte → macrophage

  • Monocytes are 2–10% of leukocytes; they enter tissues and mature into macrophages.
  • Arrive later and live long; extra weapons (NO).
  • Secrete TNF, IL-1, IL-6 and present antigen to T cells.
  • Inflammatory (M1) or repair (M2) states.
  • Tissue names: Kupffer cells (liver), alveolar macrophages (lung), microglia (brain), osteoclasts (bone).
Flag: the white-cell chart on the slide shows neutrophils at about 60% (the others: lymphocytes about 30%, monocytes about 6%, eosinophils about 3%, basophils about 1%), while the text says 40–75%. 60% sits inside that range; the chart shows a typical value and the text the normal range. The slide’s “the Marines / the Army” labels, and the note-taker’s ranking of phagocyte power and statement that monocytes are the largest circulating WBCs, are extras, not slide facts. Extra

Eosinophils, basophils, mast cells and NK cells

CellKey facts
Eosinophil1–6% of leukocytes; bilobed nucleus; red granules (major basic protein). Kill helminths; drive asthma and allergy; weak phagocytes. Targeted by anti-IL-5 drugs (mepolizumab).
Basophil and mast cellBasophils <1% in blood; mast cells live in tissues. IgE bound to FcεRI → allergen cross-links it → histamine release. Allergy and anaphylaxis (measure serum tryptase).
NK cell5–15% of blood lymphocytes; no antigen-specific receptor. Kills cells that have lost MHC I (“missing self”); kills antibody-coated cells (ADCC via CD16); secretes IFN-γ to activate macrophages.

Extra The note-taker’s summary adds: dendritic cells capture and present antigen to naïve T cells and are the most important APCs for starting primary T-cell responses; NK cells use perforin and granzymes to induce apoptosis; mast-cell degranulation causes vasodilation and increased vascular permeability.

The innate timeline (summary slide)

Barriers come first→Innate response within hours (PRRs, phagocytes, complement, cytokines, fever, acute-phase proteins)→Hand-off to adaptive immunity (dendritic cells carry antigen to lymph nodes; innate cytokines steer B and T cells)

CRP and procalcitonin measure the innate response. Breaches (burns, lines, surgery) open the door to sepsis.

Start of adaptive immunity (course “Lecture 3”, slides 1–4)

Objectives

  • Explain T-independent and T-dependent B-cell responses and the germinal centre.

Why adaptive immunity? It is the third and fourth line of defence. Most viruses and some bacteria such as TB live inside cells and escape the innate system, so adaptive immunity is needed.

The six phases of an adaptive response

PhaseWhat happens (slide details)
1 · RecognitionRare naïve clones (about 1 in 10⁵–10⁶) meet antigen in lymph nodes or spleen.
2 · ActivationAntigen plus co-stimulation plus cytokines (details in a later lecture).
3 · ExpansionCD8 clones can grow 10⁴–10⁵-fold, peaking around day 7–10.
4 · Effector phaseAntibody, cytokines and killing clear the microbe.
5 · ContractionAbout 90% of effector cells die by apoptosis once antigen falls.
6 · MemoryA larger, faster, longer-lived pool remains.
Flag: the slide shows the six phases as a graphic, and the extracted text gives the details above without phase names for each. The numbered names are the standard order. Failed contraction (FAS defects) → autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome (ALPS). The lecturer gave times for each step, which are not all visible in the text.

The B-cell receptor (BCR)

B lymphocyte→Plasma cell→Antibodies (a factory for every antibody type)
  • The BCR is membrane immunoglobulin (IgM and IgD on naïve cells) plus Igα/Igβ (CD79a/b), which carry the ITAM signalling motifs.
  • The Ig tail is too short to signal, so Igα/Igβ transmit the activation signal into the cell.
  • Every BCR on one B cell binds the same epitope.
  • B cells recognise native antigen: proteins, polysaccharides, lipids or nucleic acids, with no MHC needed.
  • Membrane Ig can also be released (secreted) into the blood and bind antigen.

CD21–CD19–CD81 co-receptor

  • CD21 binds complement fragment C3d attached to antigen. This brings antigen closer to the BCR and strongly lowers the activation threshold.
  • The CD19/CD21/CD81 complex amplifies the signal.
  • EBV uses CD21 (CR2) to enter B cells and can contribute to B-cell lymphoproliferative disorders and some lymphomas.
  • CD19 CAR-T cells and anti-CD79b polatuzumab treat B-cell lymphoma.

High-yield summary

  • Cytokines: TNF + IL-1 → endothelial activation, fever; IL-6 → fever and acute-phase proteins; CXCL8 → neutrophils; IL-12 → NK cells → IFN-γ → macrophages; type I IFN → antiviral state.
  • Cytokine storm: sepsis, CAR-T cytokine release, severe COVID-19. Drugs: anti-TNF (screen latent TB), anakinra (IL-1), tocilizumab (IL-6R).
  • Neutrophils: 40–75%; first responders; pus; neutropenia <1.5 × 10⁹/L, severe <0.5.
  • Macrophages: from monocytes (2–10%); TNF/IL-1/IL-6; antigen presentation; M1/M2; Kupffer (liver), alveolar (lung), microglia (brain), osteoclasts (bone).
  • Eosinophils: helminths, asthma/allergy, MBP; mepolizumab (anti-IL-5).
  • Basophils/mast cells: IgE–FcεRI → histamine; anaphylaxis → serum tryptase.
  • NK cells: no antigen-specific receptor; missing self (lost MHC I), ADCC via CD16, IFN-γ.
  • Innate timeline: barriers → innate response (hours) → hand-off to adaptive via dendritic cells.
  • Adaptive phases: naïve clone frequency ≈ 1 in 10⁵–10⁶; CD8 expansion 10⁴–10⁵-fold, peak day 7–10; about 90% contraction by apoptosis; memory pool remains; failed contraction (FAS) → ALPS.
  • BCR: membrane IgM/IgD + Igα/Igβ (ITAMs); recognises native antigen without MHC; one specificity per B cell. CD21 binds C3d; CD19–CD21–CD81 lowers threshold; EBV enters via CD21; CD19 CAR-T and polatuzumab (anti-CD79b) for B-cell lymphoma.

Part 1

Explanation

Antigen receptors and Ig structure

  • Antibodies (immunoglobulins) are Y-shaped proteins. The N-terminal variable domain forms the antigen-binding pocket and differs between antibodies; the C-terminal constant domain is the same within an isotype. Complement binds in the constant region, and Fc receptors on macrophages and neutrophils bind it too.
  • B-cell signalling complex: Igα and Igβ, with CD19 and CD21 as co-receptors. T cells use the CD3 complex; the TCR has α and β chains.
Flag (error in the note-taker’s page): it says the unique 3D shape of the binding pocket is called the “isotype”. The unique binding shape is the idiotype; isotype = the class of the constant region.

How millions of receptors are made from limited genes

MechanismWhat it does
V(D)J recombinationRecombinase joins variable (V), diversity (D) and joining (J) gene segments at random. In the heavy chain, D–J joins first, then V; light chains have no D segment. Defects (e.g. RAG) → SCID.
TdTTerminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase randomly inserts nucleotides at the junctions (N-nucleotide addition), per the page mainly during heavy-chain rearrangement.
Allelic exclusionOnly one of the two inherited alleles is expressed, so each cell has one receptor specificity. If the first allele makes a non-functional receptor, the cell tries the second.

Because the process is random, some products are non-functional and some react with self, which is why selection follows.

Selection of lymphocytes

  • Primary lymphoid organs (where lymphocytes develop): bone marrow (B cells) and thymus (T cells). Secondary lymphoid organs (sites of antigen exposure): spleen, lymph nodes, MALT.
  • B cells: cells with too high an affinity for normal self molecules are deleted in the bone marrow (clonal deletion) or inactivated in the periphery (anergy).
  • T cells: precursors travel from the bone marrow to the thymus cortex as double-positive (CD4⁺CD8⁺) cells. Positive selection (cortex) requires weak binding to self-MHC; cells that cannot bind die by apoptosis. Survivors go to the medulla for negative selection: strong binding to self antigen → apoptosis. The page says about 95% undergo apoptosis. The MHC type recognised decides whether the cell becomes CD4 (helper) or CD8 (cytotoxic).
Flag: the page links AIRE with negative selection of self antigens. This agrees with the slides’ statement that an AIRE defect causes APS-1, but the page’s mnemonic is not reproduced here. DiGeorge syndrome is also written next to the thymus picture without explanation.

MHC I vs MHC II

MHC IMHC II
GenesHLA-A, B, CHLA-DP, DQ, DR
Found onAll nucleated cells (including APCs)APCs only: macrophages, dendritic cells (Langerhans), B cells
Presents toCD8⁺ T cellsCD4⁺ T cells

Lymphocyte recirculation and lymphoid organs

  • Mature naïve lymphocytes recirculate through lymph nodes, spleen and MALT to maximise their chance of meeting antigen.
  • Lymph node = filter of tissues. B cells live in follicles (outer cortex): primary follicles are dense, secondary follicles are pale and have a germinal centre (where activation happens). T cells live in the paracortex. Macrophages in the medulla handle antigen.
  • Spleen = filter of blood. The marginal zone lies between white and red pulp and contains macrophages.

Immunogen, antigen and hapten

  • An immunogen must be recognised as foreign, have some chemical complexity, and a large molecular weight.
  • B cells can recognise almost any chemical; T cells recognise only peptides presented in the groove of an MHC molecule.
  • A hapten is a small molecule that cannot induce a response alone but can when coupled to a carrier protein (e.g. penicillin).
  • The response is against an epitope, not the whole structure. One epitope can be shared by more than one microorganism, which explains cross-reactivity and some false-positive serology.
Part 2

High-yield summary

  • Variable domain = antigen binding (idiotype); constant domain = class (isotype), complement and Fc receptor binding.
  • Diversity: V(D)J recombination + TdT N-nucleotides; allelic exclusion → one specificity per cell. RAG defect → SCID.
  • B cells: bone marrow deletion or anergy. T cells: thymic cortex positive selection (weak self-MHC binding), medulla negative selection (strong self binding → apoptosis).
  • MHC I (A, B, C; all nucleated cells; CD8) vs MHC II (DP, DQ, DR; APCs; CD4).
  • Primary lymphoid organs: bone marrow, thymus. Secondary: spleen, lymph nodes, MALT.
  • Lymph node: follicles (B), paracortex (T). Spleen filters blood.
  • Hapten + carrier → immunogenic (penicillin).
